\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter reports the results in two parts. The first and larger part
presents the measurements: cohort description and label variance, sample size
adequacy, internal temporal performance and calibration, clinical utility (net
benefit, operating points, event-level detection and lead time), external
validation on eICU-CRD, label-anchor attribution, the action-derived feature
ablation, model diagnostics, and the equity analysis. The second part,
Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}, assembles those measurements into the variance
decomposition and adjudicates the six pre-registered hypotheses;
Section~\ref{sec:register} then records the status of every analysis reported
here.

The verdicts come last because each is computed from quantities the earlier
sections establish, and two of them cannot be read without those quantities in
hand: H5 is a contrast between the internal and external estimates, and the
decomposition is an assembly of contrasts drawn from four different sections. A
reader who wants the answers first may begin at
Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}, but the evidence for them lies above it rather
than below.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets them, states how far each estimate can
be pushed, and sets out the limitations.

Every result below carries an explicit status tag (\textbf{[C]}
confirmatory, \textbf{[E]} exploratory, \textbf{[D]} descriptive) defined in
Section~\ref{sec:inference} and enumerated in full in
Table~\ref{tab:register}. Confirmatory results test a hypothesis whose
estimand, threshold and direction were fixed before any modelling; exploratory
results were pre-registered in scope only and generate hypotheses rather than
testing them. All intervals are 95\% percentile intervals from a stay-level
cluster bootstrap. The six pre-registered hypotheses are corrected by
Holm--Bonferroni at a family-wise error rate of 0.05; the exploratory subgroup
families are corrected by Benjamini--Hochberg at $q = 0.05$.

\section{Sample Size Adequacy \texorpdfstring{[C]}{[C]}}
\label{sec:sample_size_results}
%===========================================================================

Riley et al.'s criteria \cite{riley2019sample} are derived for a sample of
\emph{independent} observations. The modelling unit here is the person-hour,
and person-hours are not independent: they are nested within ICU stays, one per
patient. The adequacy assessment is therefore stated on the unit that carries
the information, the stay, and the person-hour count is reported
alongside it as the size of the design matrix, not as a sample size.
Table~\ref{tab:sample_size} keeps the two apart.

The corresponding design matrices contain \pcNTrainPersonHA{},
\pcNTrainPersonHB{} and \pcNTrainPersonHC{} training person-hours, at per-hour
event rates of \pcPerHourRateA{}, \pcPerHourRateB{} and \pcPerHourRateC{}. Those
figures govern the computational cost of the fit and the precision of an hourly
hazard estimate conditional on the clustering; they do not multiply the
effective sample size, and reading 2.3 million rows as 2.3 million observations
would overstate the available information by roughly the average number of
at-risk hours per stay. The relevant denominator for every inferential
statement in this thesis is the stay, which is why all intervals reported in
later sections come from a stay-level cluster bootstrap rather than a row-level
one.

Sample size is not the binding constraint on this study, and stating the
criterion on the right unit is what makes that visible: the requirement is
\pcMinNRileyStayB{} independent observations under the primary label against
\pcNTrainStays{} available, a margin of \pcRileyMarginB$\times$. Precision binds
elsewhere, where the events are, in the external cohort
(Section~\ref{sec:external_interpretability}) and in the smaller demographic
strata (Section~\ref{sec:equity}), not in the development sample.

\subsection{Key Finding: No Evaluated Operating Point Meets the Alert-Burden Benchmark}
\label{sec:utility_threshold}

At the default 0.5 cut-off no model does better than issuing no alerts. That
statement needs one immediate qualification, because the cut-off is doing much
of the work. The primary model scores \pcUtilityPrimaryB{} under Variant B not
because it alerts badly but because it never alerts: its largest predicted
hourly probability is far below 0.5, so at that cut-off it \emph{is} the
no-alert strategy and scores exactly the no-alert strategy's zero. The models
that do cross 0.5 (the rule-based scores, whose outputs are bounded integer
counts rescaled onto the unit interval, and the Cox linear predictor) cross
it so often that they score deeply negative: \pcUtilityCoxB{} for Cox and
\pcUtilityQsofaB{} for qSOFA under Variant B. A single arbitrary operating
point therefore separates the models by their calibration rather than by their
clinical value, and no conclusion about clinical failure can rest on it.

    \caption{Normalised Utility Score against alert rate for every model and
    pre-registered label variant on the temporal test set. The dashed line at
    zero is the no-alert strategy: a curve below it describes a configuration
    that would be better switched off, and points mark each model's best
    operating point. Only the primary model and GBT rise above the line at any

Four things follow, and they are more specific than a blanket verdict of
failure.

\textbf{Tuning matters, and it moves the sign.} The two fitted models cross
into positive utility once the threshold is chosen sensibly: the primary model
reaches \pcUtilityMaxPrimaryB{} under Variant B and \pcUtilityMaxPrimaryC{}
under Variant C, and GBT reaches \pcUtilityMaxGbtB{} and \pcUtilityMaxGbtC{}.
A claim that these models are worse than doing nothing would be an artefact of
the operating point, and is not made here.

\textbf{The margin is still very small.} On a scale whose upper bound is
1, the best a model achieves under the primary label is
\pcUtilityMaxGbtB{}. No minimally important difference has been established for
this score, so the gain is characterised by what it costs rather than by an
adjective: it is a shallow peak a few per cent above the no-alert strategy,
attained at \pcUtilityOptBurdenGbtB{} false alerts per true one, and measured
with the threshold selected on the same test set, so even that margin is
optimistic.

\textbf{Four of the six models cannot be tuned above zero at all.} Under
Variants A and B, NEWS2, Cox, qSOFA and SIRS have no threshold whatever at
which alerting beats not alerting; their maxima are
\pcUtilityMaxNewsB, \pcUtilityMaxCoxB, \pcUtilityMaxQsofaB{} and
\pcUtilityMaxSirsB. For NEWS2 and Cox the maximum is attained where the model
issues almost no alerts, which is the sweep's way of saying that the best
available strategy is silence. The rule-based scores in routine ward use are
in this group.

\textbf{The alarm volume is the binding constraint.} Every maximum that is
positive at all is attained at between \pcUtilityOptBurdenPrimaryC{} and
\pcUtilityOptBurdenGbtB{} false alerts per true alert, twenty to
thirty-seven times the benchmark of 1.4 \cite{moor2023review}, and the two
fitted models detect only \pcUtilityOptDetectPctPrimaryB{} to
\pcUtilityOptDetectPctGbtC{} per cent of septic stays in the actionable window
there. The Utility Score's own optimum is a configuration that misses most
septic patients and alarms constantly on the rest. That, rather than the sign
of a score at an arbitrary cut-off, is what the evidence supports.

This is consistent with Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2025srpm}: the
internal-to-external utility collapse they found across ninety-one models
already has its internal counterpart on MIMIC-IV under an honest time-based
split, in the form of an optimum that sits barely above zero before the model
leaves its development cohort. The failure is only visible because the Utility
Score is reported; AUROC alone would hide it, and Table~\ref{tab:internal_results}
shows discrimination in the high 0.7s for the same configurations.

One further qualification is developed in Section~\ref{sec:clinical_utility}.
The Utility Score and decision-curve net benefit disagree here because they
price false alarms differently, at a threshold matched to the event
prevalence the primary model does achieve positive net benefit over both
default strategies (Table~\ref{tab:net_benefit}). That disagreement shows the
verdict to be sensitive to the metric chosen, which is the concern H5 was
written to address, but it is an internal contrast between two metrics and not
a test of H5's internal-to-external estimand. It is a reason to report both
numbers rather than to prefer one. The
conclusion that survives both metrics is the alarm-volume one: no
configuration tested reaches an alert burden a ward could absorb.

\subsection{Calibration \texorpdfstring{[C]}{[C]}}

Calibration intercept and slope are two summary numbers describing a whole
curve, so both the curve itself and a scalar summary of its distance from the
diagonal are reported. Following Van Calster et al.\
\cite{vancalster2019calibration}, the flexible calibration curve is a
restricted cubic spline of the outcome on the logit of predicted risk, and it
is summarised by the Integrated Calibration Index (ICI, the mean absolute
distance between predicted risk and that curve) and the scaled Brier score,
$1 - \text{Brier}/\text{Brier}_{\text{null}}$, where the null model predicts
the prevalence for every person-hour.

Two results stand out. First, only the primary model is calibrated at all, and
it is calibrated under \emph{every} label variant: its intercept sits within
\pcCalibIntMaxAbsPrimary{} of zero, its slope within
\pcCalibSlopeMaxDevPrimary{} of one
(\pcCalibSlopePrimaryA, \pcCalibSlopePrimaryB, \pcCalibSlopePrimaryC), and its
largest ICI across the three variants is \pcIciPrimaryC{}, under a fifth of
the 0.2 per cent event rate.
Its scaled Brier score is positive under all three, though barely
(\pcBrierScaledPrimaryA{} to \pcBrierScaledPrimaryC), meaning it improves on
the null model by less than one per cent. Calibration this close to the diagonal
at this event rate is the result that makes the model's outputs usable as
probabilities at all, and it is what distinguishes it from every comparator
here.

Second, GBT and Cox are not merely imperfectly calibrated; they are
miscalibrated by roughly three orders of magnitude. Both carry intercepts near
$-6$, and their scaled Brier scores run into the tens and hundreds below zero.
A scaled Brier of \pcBrierScaledGbtB{} means the GBT model's squared error is
some ninety-eight times worse than simply predicting the prevalence for every
person-hour. Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}
shows why: GBT emits predicted risks between 0.2 and 0.9 for person-hours
whose observed event rate never exceeds 0.04. This matters directly for
Section~\ref{sec:clinical_utility}: a model whose probabilities are inflated by
two orders of magnitude cannot be operated at a threshold chosen from clinical
reasoning about risk, because its outputs do not mean what they appear to mean.
Its useful operating points exist, GBT's best lies near
\pcUtilityOptThresholdGbtB{} under Variant B
(Table~\ref{tab:utility_optimum}), but they can only be located empirically,
by sweeping, and they carry no interpretation as a risk a clinician has agreed
to act on.

The consistency across variants is itself worth noting. Calibration is the one
respect in which the three label definitions make no difference at all: slopes
of \pcCalibSlopePrimaryA, \pcCalibSlopePrimaryB{} and \pcCalibSlopePrimaryC{}
and $E_{\max}$ values of \pcEmaxPrimaryA, \pcEmaxPrimaryB{} and
\pcEmaxPrimaryC{} are indistinguishable for practical purposes. Whatever the
label changes about which patients are septic, it does not change how faithfully
the fitted hazard tracks the observed rate.

\subsubsection{A Correction to the Clamped Estimates}

The calibration intercepts and slopes above supersede those produced by the
metric-suite notebook, which computed them after clamping predicted
probabilities to $[0.001, 0.999]$. That guard is standard for models whose
predictions approach 0 or 1, but at a 0.2 per cent hourly event rate it is
actively harmful: \pcPctBelowClampPrimaryA\% (Variant A),
\pcPctBelowClampPrimaryB\% (Variant B) and \pcPctBelowClampPrimaryC\%
(Variant C) of the primary model's predicted probabilities fall \emph{below}
the clamp floor, so the clamp was overwriting a large share of the quantity
being measured. No predicted probability exceeds \pcMaxPredPrimaryC{} under any
variant, so the
ceiling never binds; it is only the floor that does damage. The
values in Table~\ref{tab:calibration} use a floor of $10^{-6}$, below the
smallest non-zero prediction in the test set. Under the conventional clamp the primary
model's Variant A slope reads \pcCalibSlopeClampedPrimaryA{} against
\pcCalibSlopePrimaryA{} on the
$10^{-6}$ floor: the clamp makes a well-calibrated model look materially
worse than it is. Both figures are given rather than only the second, because
the gap between them is exactly the kind of scale-dependent analyst decision
this study set out to measure: a calibration guard chosen without reference to
the event rate is itself a source of reported variance.

\section{Clinical Utility: Net Benefit, Operating Points, and Event-Level
    Detection}
\label{sec:clinical_utility}
%===========================================================================

AUROC, AUPRC and the Utility Score describe a model; they do not describe what
a ward would experience if the model were switched on. This section reports
the four quantities that do: net benefit across the range of thresholds a
clinician might plausibly accept, the positive predictive value and false-alarm
rate at a fixed sensitivity, detection expressed per patient rather than per
person-hour, and the lead time gained when detection occurs.

One framing point governs everything below. Under the discrete-time
competing-risks specification, $\text{event}=1$ marks the sepsis onset hour
itself, so each septic stay contributes exactly one event hour out of a mean
of 45. Per-hour sensitivity therefore demands that the alert fire \emph{in the
onset hour}; per-patient sensitivity asks only whether the patient was alerted
at all before onset. The two are different questions, and reporting only the
first, as the metric suite in Section~\ref{sec:internal_performance} does,
understates detection while hiding the alarm volume that buys it.

\subsection{Decision-Curve Analysis \texorpdfstring{[C]}{[C]}}

Decision-curve analysis, pre-specified in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, requires a choice of
threshold grid, and at this prevalence that choice determines the entire
result. A threshold probability of $t$ encodes a willingness to accept
$(1-t)/t$ false alarms per true detection. The hourly event rate here is
0.19--0.42 per cent, so the conventional grid of 0.01--0.20 sits between five
and one hundred times the prevalence: at $t=0.01$ the analyst is asserting a
tolerance of only 99 false alarms per detection, far below what an hourly
sepsis alert must absorb, and every curve falls trivially below ``treat
none''. Net benefit is therefore evaluated on a grid running from one tenth to
ten times the prevalence, following Vickers and Elkin \cite{vickers2006dca}.
The first run of the metric suite used the conventional grid and produced no
usable curve; that null result is a property of the grid, not of the models.

Read on the correct threshold scale, decision-curve analysis gives the most
favourable result in this thesis, and it is still a weak one. At a
threshold equal to the prevalence, the primary model captures
\pcSnbPctPrimaryA\% (Variant A), \pcSnbPctPrimaryB\% (Variant B) and
\pcSnbPctPrimaryC\% (Variant C) of the attainable net benefit. The three
variants agree closely, spanning barely three percentage points, so
unlike discrimination, where the label moves the answer, the share of
attainable benefit captured is insensitive to which reading of Sepsis-3 is
used. This is genuine, if modest, value over both defaults, and it is the same
qualitative verdict the swept Utility Score reaches in
Table~\ref{tab:utility_optimum}: a small positive margin for the fitted models
once the threshold is set on the right scale, and nothing at all for the
rule-based comparators. The two metrics still differ in magnitude, because they
price errors differently: net benefit weights a false alarm by $t/(1-t)$, which
at $t = \pcPrevalenceB$ is almost nothing, whereas the Utility Score imposes a
fixed $-0.05$ penalty per false-alert hour regardless of threshold. That is why
net benefit is the more generous of the two here, and why the gap between them
widens as the alert rate rises. Which pricing is right is a clinical judgement,
not a statistical one. This internal disagreement demonstrates sensitivity to
metric choice, which is the concern H5 was written to address, but it is not an
instance of H5's estimand: that contrast is an internal-to-external comparison,
not a comparison between two metrics inside one cohort.

The alert-rate span column carries the more damaging finding. For every model
except the primary one it is exactly zero: across the entire plausible
threshold range, the alert rate does not move. GBT and Cox alert on
\emph{100\% of person-hours} at every threshold in this range, so their
decision curves are
not merely poor but identical to treating everyone: the green GBT line in
Figure~\ref{fig:decision_curves} sits exactly on the treat-all reference.
This is the direct consequence of the calibration failure in
Table~\ref{tab:calibration}: with predicted probabilities inflated to the
0.2--0.9 range, no threshold below 4 per cent excludes anything. NEWS2, qSOFA
and SIRS are non-tunable for the opposite reason (their integer scores map
to a handful of fixed probabilities, all above the grid), and alert on a
constant 58--86 per cent of person-hours. The primary model is the only one of
the six that can be operated as a threshold-based alert at all.

\section{External Validation on eICU-CRD \texorpdfstring{[C]}{[C]}}
\label{sec:external_validation}
\label{sec:external}
%===========================================================================

External validation asks whether a frozen model ranks person-hours as well in a
different health system as it does in the one it was fitted on. Answering it
requires transporting the label as well as the model, and in this pair of
databases the label is by far the harder of the two. This section therefore
reports the labelling inputs first, then discrimination, then the operating
characteristics a ward would experience, because each is only readable in the
light of the one before it.

\subsection{What the Sepsis-3 Anchor Can Reach in eICU-CRD}
\label{sec:external_coverage}

Sepsis-3 onset is anchored on the co-occurrence of an antibiotic order and a
microbiology culture. In eICU-CRD the culture limb is documented for
\pcExtMicroCoverage\% of the analysis cohort (\pcExtNMicroStays{} of
\pcExtNCohortStays{} unit stays), against near-universal coverage in MIMIC-IV,
while antibiotic exposure, pooled across the \texttt{medication} and
\texttt{infusionDrug} tables, reaches \pcExtAbxCoverage\% of stays. The
asymmetry is decisive: in a stay with no culture record the Sepsis-3
conjunction cannot fire under \emph{any} variant, whatever the patient's
physiology.

Coverage of a single limb, however, is an upper bound on what the conjunction
can reach rather than a description of it, and the two quantities differ by an
order of magnitude. Requiring \emph{both} limbs to be documented at all, at
any offset and before any window is imposed, leaves \pcExtNBothLimbStays{}
stays, \pcExtBothLimbCoverage\% of the cohort. Requiring them to co-occur
inside a variant's own antibiotic--culture window, which is what suspicion of
infection means, leaves \pcExtNSuspicionA{}, \pcExtNSuspicionB{} and
\pcExtNSuspicionC{} stays under Variants A, B and C. Adding the
organ-dysfunction criterion leaves \pcExtNOnsetStaysA{},
\pcExtNOnsetStaysB{} and \pcExtNOnsetStaysC{} labelled stays.

One further step separates the labelled stays from the events the external
AUROC is computed on, and it accounts for the only discrepancy between the two:
under Variant B, \pcExtNOnsetStaysB{} stays are labelled but
\pcExtNEventsB{} events are scored. An onset falling after a stay's last
observed hour cannot be predicted from inside that stay's panel, so it is
treated as right-censoring rather than as an event, the same horizon rule the
development cohort applies at 72 hours. Two of Variant B's onsets fall that way;
none of Variant A's or Variant C's do, which is why only Variant B moves between
the two tables. No labelled onset hour is lost for any other reason: every one
that falls inside its panel carries a complete covariate vector and receives a
prediction from all three models.

The four coverage counts
answer four different questions and are stated together here because a single
percentage cannot carry the constraint: \pcExtMicroCoverage\% is the sub-cohort
in which an onset is \emph{observable}, and it is the denominator of the
external analysis; \pcExtBothLimbCoverage\% is the ceiling on how many stays
the conjunction could ever label; and the labelled counts are what the AUROC
estimates actually rest on.

This has a direct consequence for how an external event rate may be read.
Applying the conjunction across all \pcExtPanelHoursFull{} person-hours would
place the overwhelming majority of the denominator in stays where the numerator
is structurally zero, and the resulting rate would describe the completeness of
one source table rather than the incidence of sepsis. The primary external
analysis is therefore confined to the \pcExtPanelHoursMicro{} person-hours in
which an onset is observable in principle.

inputs.

A gap of this size is a substantive finding about how differently the two
systems document infection, and it is the reason the restricted cohort is the
right denominator. A gap of two or more orders of magnitude would not be a
finding at all: no plausible difference in case mix separates two large adult
ICU populations by that much, and such a gap would indicate that the labelling
inputs, not the patients, differ.

\textbf{The point estimate falls under every label.} The primary
model falls from \pcAurocPrimaryA{} to \pcExtAurocPrimaryA{} (Narrow), from

Seymour-Standard contrast carries an interval, because only it is the tested
contrast of H5, and that interval covers zero
(Section~\ref{sec:h5}); the Narrow and Liberal figures are point estimates on
\pcExtNEventsA{} and \pcExtNEventsC{} events with no interval attached, so
``under every label'' describes the direction of three estimates and not three
established degradations. The Seymour-Standard
drop of \pcHFiveEst{} is about three-quarters of the 0.085 that Moor et al.\

approximately met on the point estimate. Section~\ref{sec:external_interpretability}
sets out why it is not established at conventional confidence.

The point estimates are ordered by label in the same direction as the internal
results: the narrowest label falls furthest and the most liberal least. Only
the Seymour-Standard contrast carries an interval, so an ordering of three
noisy estimates is a consistency observation rather than evidence that the
label governs transport; it is reported because the antibiotic-only arm
reproduces it on two orders of magnitude more events
(Section~\ref{sec:external_interpretability}).

NEWS2 degrades further and faster, to \pcExtAurocNewsB{} under the primary
label: effectively chance. On this arm, then, the fitted models retain more of
their advantage over a bedside score than the score retains of its own internal
performance. That is the one comparison in this section that favours the fitted
model, and it should not be carried further than the arm it is computed on:
Section~\ref{sec:external_interpretability} reports the same three comparators
under the antibiotic-only anchor, where the event count is two orders of
magnitude larger and the ordering between the boosted model and NEWS2 does not
hold.

Two further properties of the table are diagnostic rather than substantive, and
both are worth stating because their violation would be invisible otherwise.
First, the event counts differ across variants. They must: the variants differ
in their antibiotic--culture windows, so they select different stays, and an
external analysis reporting one event count for all three would be reporting a
single label applied three times. Second, NEWS2's AUROC differs across
variants. Its \emph{predictor} is label-invariant, the score is a function of
physiology alone and is computed once, but its AUROC is not, because AUROC is
a property of the predictor \emph{and} the outcome it is ranked against. An
identical NEWS2 AUROC across variants is therefore a signature of an unvaried
label, and the pipeline halts on it rather than reporting it.

Every external event count above falls below the interpretability floor adopted
in this study. Section~\ref{sec:external_interpretability} states what that
permits the external estimates to support, and reports the antibiotic-only
sensitivity arm that bounds them.

\section{Label-Anchor Attribution: Definitional or Empirical?
\texorpdfstring{[E]}{[E]}}
\label{sec:label_anchor_results}
%===========================================================================

Everything to this point varies the label \emph{within} Sepsis-3. Variants A, B
and C are three readings of one definition and all three fire on the same
culture-plus-antibiotic anchor, so H1 cannot distinguish two very different
claims: that the reading of a definition matters (definitional), or that the
choice of construct matters (empirical). This section separates them by
splitting the Sepsis-3 conjunction into its two limbs and labelling each on its
own (Section~\ref{sec:alt_labels}). It is post-hoc, under Protocol
Amendment 2, and its contrasts form exploratory family E3.

\subsection{The Anchor Determines Every Onset Time}

The first result is structural rather than statistical, and it can be stated
exactly. Under variants A, B and C the onset time \emph{is} the suspicion time
$t_{\text{susp}}$; the SOFA criterion decides only \emph{whether} a stay is
labelled, never \emph{when}. Variant E, which removes the SOFA criterion,
therefore turns out to be a strict superset of Variant B: every one of B's
\pcNPositiveBPanel{} labelled stays is also labelled by E, and in
\textbf{\pcPctExactMatchE\% of them the onset hour is identical}. Adding the
organ-dysfunction requirement discards \pcNPositiveEExcessOverB{} of E's
\pcNPositiveE{} labelled stays, two thirds, and moves not one onset
by a single hour.

different times.

different clock. It labels every stay B labels ($\kappa = \pcKappaVsBF$, the
shortfall being the \pcFNExtraInHorizon{} stays discussed above whose onset only

in just \pcPctExactMatchF\% of them. \textbf{A quarter of Sepsis-3 onsets are
placed at a different hour by two defensible readings of the same definition, on
the same patients.} Neither the pre-registered variants nor Variant D could
expose that, because the first hold timing fixed and the second changes
everything at once.

\paragraph{The anchor-attributable fraction is indistinguishable from one on the primary model.}

cohort the treatment anchor alone is about as predictable as Sepsis-3 itself,
neither more nor less. Dropping the organ-dysfunction requirement nearly
triples the event count (\pcAltNEventsE{} against \pcNTestEventsB) and leaves
discrimination essentially where it was. The SOFA gate selects which stays are
labelled without selecting the separable ones, so ``how much of the model's
performance is attributable to the treatment anchor?'' has the answer
``essentially all of it, but the anchor is not itself the easy part''.

\paragraph{What does separate is the anchor-independent label.}
Variant D is the one contrast that moves: $\Delta = \pcAltDeltaPrimaryD$
[\pcAltDeltaCiLoPrimaryD, \pcAltDeltaCiHiPrimaryD] for the primary model and
$\pcAltDeltaGbtD$ for GBT, both significant in family E3. Its AUPRC gap is
larger still (\pcAltAuprcFullPrimaryD{} against \pcAuprcPrimaryB), which matters
more at this prevalence. The caveat of Section~\ref{sec:alt_labels} governs the
\emph{level}: D's label is a threshold function of physiology of which five of
six components are model inputs, so part of that separation is self-fulfilling.

\paragraph{And so does moving the clock.}
Variant F, whose label is Sepsis-3 unmodified in membership, is also
significantly more separable than B ($\Delta = \pcAltDeltaPrimaryF$
[\pcAltDeltaCiLoPrimaryF, \pcAltDeltaCiHiPrimaryF],
$p_{\text{BH}} = \pcAltDeltaPBhPrimaryF$). Its AUPRC is \emph{higher} than B's
despite a comparable event count, so this is not a prevalence artefact. Onsets
placed at the moment organ dysfunction is first detectable are more separable
from their surrounding at-risk hours than the same onsets placed at the moment
a clinician acted. That is consistent with the physiological signal preceding
the clinical response, but it does not establish it: the estimand is
onset-hour discrimination and its predictors are binned to the same hour as the
onset (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), so an ordering in time is compatible
with the result rather than demonstrated by it.

\paragraph{Where this leaves the difficulty.}
Sepsis-3 is not harder to predict than its limbs; it is about as hard as its
anchor limb, easier than its physiological limb, and harder than itself under a
better clock. The difficulty of sepsis onset prediction on MIMIC-IV therefore
does not live in the conjunction as such. It lives in \emph{when} the
conjunction is stamped, at the clinician's action rather than at the
patient's deterioration, and that is a choice the definition leaves open and
this literature has largely closed one way.

\subsection{The Empty Pre-Treatment Window Is a Property of the Onset Rule}
\label{sec:pretreatment_label}

Section~\ref{sec:h3} proved that the pre-treatment window must be empty under
the onset rule variants A--C use. This section reports what is in that window
once the rule is changed, and separates three distinct rows.

Two of the four rows carry no information. Variant E's zero is a tautology
statable without any data: E's onset \emph{is} the anchor, so the pre-anchor
window is empty by construction. Variant B's zero is the same tautology one step
removed: B is a subset of E with identical onset times, so if E cannot fire
before the anchor, neither can B.

\paragraph{Variant F: the same label, timed as the standard specifies.}
F is the row that answers the question directly, because it changes one thing.
The suspicion windows, the SOFA threshold and the evaluation window are Variant
B's; the septic stays are Variant B's, stay for stay: all \pcFNOnsets{} of
them. Only the timestamp differs. On that single change, \pcFNMovedEarlier{}
onsets move earlier (\pcFPctMovedEarlier\%; median \pcFMedianShiftH\,h, largest
\pcFMaxShiftH\,h), \pcFNBeforeAnchor{} land before the first antibiotic or
culture across the cohort, and \pcPreTreatOnsetsVarF{} of those fall in the
temporal test set. \textbf{Sepsis-3 onsets can and do precede clinical action.}
The primary model identifies those hours at \pcPreTreatAurocPrimaryF{} AUROC.

This is the number the pre-registered analysis could not produce, and it is
independent of the Variant D caveat below: F's label is Sepsis-3, unmodified in
membership, so nothing about it is a different construct.

\paragraph{Onset timing also changes how many events exist at all.}
Agreement between F and B is \pcPctAgreeBF\% ($\kappa = \pcKappaVsBF$) rather
than exact, and the discrepancy is one-directional and informative. Within the
72-hour prediction horizon F labels \pcNPositiveF{} stays against B's
\pcNPositiveB, and every one of B's is among them: $F \supseteq B$. The
\pcFNExtraInHorizon{} additional stays are those whose $t_{\text{susp}}$ falls
after hour 72, so no model here ever sees a labelled event for them, but
whose SOFA rise occurred inside the window. Standard timing makes them
modellable; conjunction timing discards them. The onset rule therefore governs
not only \emph{when} events are recorded but \emph{how many} a real-time model
can be trained and tested on at all, which is a second and quite separate route
by which an operationalisation choice propagates into reported performance.

\paragraph{Variant D: a different construct, and a larger count.}
Variant D's \pcPreTreatOnsetsVarD{} pre-treatment onsets are consistent with F
and larger, as expected of a label that drops the infection requirement
entirely. A model identifies those hours at \pcPreTreatAurocPrimaryD{} AUROC
(\pcPreTreatAurocGbtD{} for GBT). The caveat of Section~\ref{sec:alt_labels}
applies to that \emph{level}, D's label is a threshold function of
physiology of which most components are model inputs, but not to the event
count.

\paragraph{The form contribution C2 takes.}
C2 is deliberately \emph{not} the proposition that no patient develops sepsis
before clinicians act. That proposition is unsupportable in general and the
external literature contradicts it: the Epic Sepsis Model v2 evaluation
\cite{wong2026epic} reports median lead times of 1.4--7.1 hours ahead of
clinician recognition across four sites at encounter AUROC 0.80--0.90. C2 is
instead a two-part claim, one part analytic and one measured, and the two parts
are load-bearing in different ways:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Analytic.} Under a conjunction-based onset rule (the rule
          used by the pre-registered variants here and, in the author's reading,
          by a substantial share of the MIMIC-IV sepsis literature)
          pre-treatment prediction is structurally impossible.
          Proposition~\ref{prop:h3} is the proof. Any study reporting
          pre-treatment performance on such a label is reporting an empty set,
          and any study \emph{not} restricting the window is, to that extent,
          scoring discrimination around a clinician-defined decision, inside
          a window that decision opened.
    \item \textbf{Measured.} Restoring the standard timing branch recovers
          \pcPreTreatOnsetsVarF{} pre-treatment onsets on the identical cohort,
          separable from their surrounding at-risk hours at
          \pcPreTreatAurocPrimaryF{} AUROC. The events exist and are
          representable; it is the operationalisation, not the data, that
          removes them. Whether they are predictable \emph{in advance} is a
          further question this design cannot answer, because the predictors
          for an onset hour are binned to that same hour
          (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\end{enumerate}

Sepsis-3 \emph{membership} remains constituted by clinical action under every
timing rule, without a qualifying antibiotic-culture pair there is no label
at all, so Kamran et al.'s \cite{kamran2024evaluation} concern survives at the
level of which patients can ever enter the positive class. The remedy
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} identifies is unchanged in direction and weaker in
force: change the label, and at minimum time it as the standard requires.

Variant D's discrimination is not comparable with Variant B's, for reasons
fixed in advance by its construction; what the comparison does and does not
license is set out in Section~\ref{sec:variant_d_establishes}.

\section{Model Diagnostics}
\label{sec:model_diagnostics}
%===========================================================================

Two checks on the comparator models are collected here rather than reported
beside the results they qualify, because neither feeds a hypothesis and neither
is read on its own. The first tests the assumption the static Cox comparator
rests on, and so establishes whether that comparator is a fair one; the second
describes which predictors the gradient-boosted trees actually used, and so
bears on the ablation in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} and on the coefficient
instability in Section~\ref{sec:h2}.

\subsection{Proportional-Hazards Testing \texorpdfstring{[C]}{[C]}}
\label{sec:schoenfeld}

The Schoenfeld residual test on the static Cox model (Variant B) decisively
rejects proportional hazards ($\chi^2 = \pcSchoenfeldChisq$,
df = \pcSchoenfeldDf, $p = \pcSchoenfeldP$).
\pcSchoenfeldNViolate{} of the \pcSchoenfeldNCovariates{} covariates violate
the assumption individually at $\alpha = 0.05$, led by GCS, bilirubin,
respiratory rate and creatinine. These are per-covariate tests reported without
multiplicity adjustment; the global test alone is sufficient for the
pre-registered check, and the smallest p-values survive Bonferroni correction
over the covariate set by many orders of magnitude. The assumption the static
Cox comparator rests on therefore fails on these data, so its coefficients
cannot be read as time-constant hazard ratios, and the discrete-time
formulation is the better-founded choice for the primary model.

\section{Equity Analysis \texorpdfstring{[E]}{[E]}}
\label{sec:equity}

\subsection{What the Subgroup Strata Are}
\label{sec:equity_strata}

Two properties of the stratifying variables govern how everything in this
section may be read, and both are properties of MIMIC-IV's administrative
record rather than of the analysis.

\paragraph{The strata are documentation categories, not populations.}
\texttt{race} in MIMIC-IV is a single code assigned at registration. Its levels
mix broad categories with more granular ones drawn from the same population:
``White'' and ``White -- Other European'' are separate levels, as are ``Asian''
and ``Asian -- Chinese''. A stay carries exactly one code, so the levels
partition the cohort, but they do not partition it into disjoint
\emph{populations}: the reference level ``White'' is a residual category
comprising stays not assigned a more specific European code, and a contrast
against it is a contrast against that residual, not against White patients in
general. ``Unknown'' and ``Unable to obtain'' record the \emph{absence} of an
answer. They are retained because the completeness of the demographic record is
itself informative, Section~\ref{sec:label_sens_subgroup} shows why, but
they are not demographic groups and no result concerning them is a result about
a population.

\paragraph{An event floor is applied before anything is interpreted.}
A subgroup AUROC estimated on a few dozen onsets is not a quantity about which
anything can be said. The floor adopted here is \pcMinSubgroupEvents{} sepsis
onsets, the same threshold and the same warrant as the external-validation
floor of Section~\ref{sec:external_interpretability}
\cite{riley2019sample, collins2024tripodai}. Strata below it are estimated and
tabulated, so that the reader can see which groups exist in the cohort and how
few events they carry, but they are \textbf{not interpreted}, are excluded from
every statement about spread, and are excluded from the exploratory contrast
family E1, which therefore contains \pcEquityNContrastsEOne{} contrasts, not
one per level. Of the racial strata, \pcNSubgroupsAboveFloor{} clear the floor.

Read as a list of point estimates the table appears to show a wide spread, and
it is tempting to set that spread beside the model-class and label spreads of
Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}. It should not be. The three quantities are not
commensurable, for three reasons that are independent of each other. They are
different estimands: the decomposition rows are contrasts computed on the whole
test cohort with one factor varied and everything else held fixed, whereas a
subgroup range is the extreme order statistic of a set of separate estimates
computed on disjoint slices of that cohort. They rest on different amounts of
information: the decomposition rows use every event in the test set, while the
subgroup estimates divide those events between strata, the smallest of which
carry a few dozen. And the range of a set of noisy estimates is biased upward
by construction, it grows with the number of strata and with their
imprecision even when every underlying value is identical, so it is not an
effect size at all.

What the intervals show is that the apparent spread is largely a function of
how few events the small strata contain. Of the racial strata,
\pcNSubgroupsAboveFloor{} carry at least \pcMinSubgroupEvents{} onsets; the
remaining \pcEquityRaceBelowFloorN{} are estimated on counts between
\pcEquityRaceBelowFloorMin{} and \pcEquityRaceBelowFloorMax, and their
intervals are correspondingly wide.

    read here. The reference level is the largest stratum of its own variable
    and, for race, is a residual administrative category rather than a
    population (Section~\ref{sec:equity_strata}). \textbf{[E] exploratory.}}
    \label{tab:equity_contrasts}
\end{table}

Applying the floor leaves \pcEquityNContrastsEOne{} contrasts in family E1
across all three equity variables and both models, and \textbf{none survives
false-discovery correction}. The smallest adjusted value in the family belongs
to a contrast on insurance rather than on race: \pcEquityTopEOneLevel{}
insurance against the Medicare reference, \pcEquityTopEOneDelta{} AUROC for the
gradient-boosted model, with a bootstrap interval of
[\pcEquityTopEOneCiLo, \pcEquityTopEOneCiHi] and
$q_{\text{BH}} = \pcEquityTopEOneQ$.

That row is worth pausing on, because it is where an unadjusted interval and a
corrected verdict part company. The interval excludes zero; the adjusted
$p$-value does not clear the threshold. Both statements are correct, and the
second is the one this study reads, because the contrast was not pre-specified:
§9 of the protocol fixed the scope of the equity analysis but neither the
levels, nor the reference, nor the direction of any contrast, so an uncorrected
interval selected after the fact from six is not evidence of the strength it
appears to have. The contrast is reported rather than suppressed, and it is not
claimed. Reading it as a finding would be exactly the practice this thesis
objects to elsewhere.

One consequence deserves stating plainly, because it bounds the whole analysis.
Of the racial strata, only two carry enough events to be interpreted, and one of
them is \emph{Unknown}: a missingness category rather than a population
(Section~\ref{sec:equity_strata}). \textbf{This study therefore contains no
sufficiently powered comparison between two racial strata that both record an
actual demographic answer.} That is not a null result about racial disparity in
sepsis prediction; it is the absence of the measurement that would be needed to
have one.

The correct reading of Table~\ref{tab:equity_contrasts} is accordingly that this
study is \textbf{underpowered to detect subgroup disparities}, not that no
disparities exist. Even among the strata clearing the event floor the intervals
are wide enough to be compatible with a disparity larger than any effect
reported elsewhere in this thesis, so the data are consistent both with a
stratum being served materially worse than the reference and with its being
served slightly better. Non-significance here is a statement about precision and
nothing else.

Wang et al.'s \cite{wang2022equity} finding of degraded performance for Asian,
Hispanic and Spanish-speaking patients is therefore neither confirmed nor
contradicted. It could not be: that result concerns mortality prediction among
patients already identified as septic, not onset prediction
(Section~\ref{sec:equity_lit}), and the strata in which it would have to be
checked here are precisely the ones that fall below the event floor. The point
estimates for the smaller strata do sit below the reference under GBT, in the
direction Wang et al.\ report, but they are not interpreted and no inference is
drawn from them.

($\pcLabelSensPrivatePp$ pp, $q = \pcLabelSensPrivateQ$).

That composition dictates the reading, and it is narrower than a
racial-disparity reading would be. What varies with the label definition is
concentrated in the stays whose demographic record is incomplete, together with
two strata, non-English language and insurance type, that index access to
the care pathway rather than the patient's physiology. The coherent
interpretation is about \textbf{documentation}, not about race: a stay whose
registration record is incomplete is a stay whose clinical record is also more
likely to be incomplete, and because the Sepsis-3 label is constituted by two
documented clinician actions (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}), incomplete
documentation propagates directly into unstable labelling. Loosening the
definition recovers proportionally more of those stays than of well-documented
ones.

This is a finding about the label's dependence on recording practice, and it is
worth stating in that form because it is actionable in a way a racial-disparity
finding computed on these strata would not be: it identifies the mechanism, documentation completeness, rather than the demographic correlate. Whether
that mechanism is itself differentially distributed across patient populations
is a question these strata cannot answer, for the reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:equity_strata}, and this thesis does not answer it.

Of the two equity analyses, this is the one with the precision to support any
claim at all. The discrimination analysis does not, and the pairing is the
point: had only the discrimination analysis been reported, its wide intervals
would have been read as an absence of disparity; had only the label analysis
been reported, its significant contrasts would have been read as a demographic
disparity. Reported together, each bounds how the other may be read.

To the best of our knowledge, bounded by the narrative search documented in

the pairing, and the pairing is what makes the two results separable here: only
one of them is precise enough to act on, and that is not knowable from either
in isolation.

\section{Variance Decomposition and Hypothesis Verdicts \texorpdfstring{[C]}{[C]}}
\label{sec:decomposition}
%===========================================================================

This section adjudicates the research question and the six pre-registered
hypotheses. It introduces no new measurement: every estimate below is assembled
from quantities the preceding sections establish, which is why it comes last.
The label spread is read across the three variants of
Section~\ref{sec:internal_performance}, the model-class spread across the model
rows of the same table, the split contrast against the random-split arm, and
the transport contrast against the external estimates of
Section~\ref{sec:external_validation}. Assembling them in one place is what
makes the four analyst decisions commensurable; each is a contrast in AUROC on
the same scale, and each carries an interval from the same stay-level cluster
bootstrap.

\paragraph{What a spread is here, and what the choice costs.} Each spread is a
\emph{range}: the largest AUROC in a set minus the smallest. The label spread is
therefore a range over three variants and the model-class spread a range over
six model specifications, and that asymmetry has to be declared, because
Section~\ref{sec:equity} rejects the subgroup range as an effect size on exactly
this ground. A range grows with the number of values it is taken over even when
the underlying quantities are identical, so a range over six is expected to
exceed a range over three by roughly half again. The bootstrap does not remove
this: the bias is present in every replicate.

But the ordering does not rest on the statistic. The model-class range
spans a structural gap rather than sampling scatter, between the two fitted
models and the four rule-based and static comparators, and \emph{any} single
contrast across that gap is larger than the whole label range. The narrowest of
them, GBT against the Cox model, is \pcAurocGbtB{} against \pcAurocCoxB, which
exceeds the label range by a factor of eight and involves no order statistic at
all. An asymmetry worth about half again cannot account for a difference of
\pcSpreadModelClass{} against \pcSpreadLabel.

A variance-components partition would be the more usual instrument, and it is
not used here for two reasons. The AUROCs being compared are computed on the
same test rows rather than on independent samples, so the design does not supply
the error term such a model needs. And \S7 of the pre-registration fixed the
estimand as the \emph{spread} before any modelling; replacing it now with a
variance component would substitute a post-hoc quantity for a pre-registered
one, which is the practice this thesis objects to elsewhere. The range is
reported as what it is, a descriptive summary carrying a bootstrap interval,
rather than as a partition of variance.

Two of the six hypotheses turn out not to admit a test statistic at all, for
reasons that are properties of their design rather than of the data. Those are
stated where each is reported, and the consequence for the correction (that both still consume a slot in the family, which makes the adjustment on the
remaining four stricter) is set out in
Section~\ref{sec:confirmatory_verdicts}.

The decomposition does not distribute variance evenly across the four analyst
decisions, and the imbalance is the result, though not the imbalance the
study anticipated. \textbf{Model class dominates} (\pcSpreadModelClass).
\textbf{External transport has the second-largest point estimate}
(\pcSpreadMetric), five times the label definition's, but its interval is the
widest in the table and covers zero, so its rank relative to the other factors
is not established. The label itself moves discrimination by
\pcSpreadLabel{} and the choice of split by \pcSpreadSplit{}, both small,
and the split's interval covers zero comfortably.

The ordering matters more than the individual magnitudes, and it is only
partly established. The two decisions this literature scrutinises most closely,
how the label is read and how the data are split, are the two that move
discrimination least, and both are measured on the full test cohort.
\emph{Where the model is deployed} carries the second-largest point estimate,
several times \emph{how sepsis is defined}, but its interval is too wide to
establish that rank or that it moves AUROC more than the label does. What the
decomposition settles is that the label and the split move discrimination
little; what it leaves open is how much the destination population moves it.

The study was designed on the expectation that these four would be comparable,
and that expectation is what fails. Three qualifications keep the result from
being read too broadly:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The model-class spread compares six estimators ranging from a
          six-item bedside score to a 29-term hazard model, so it is a
          heterogeneous factor by construction. Between the two \emph{flexible}
          estimators the gap is \pcHSixAbsEst{}, an order of magnitude below
          the six-class range and of the same order as the label's
          (H6, non-inferiority established in the direction that matters).
    \item The label factor is small \emph{in discrimination}, which is not the
          same as small in consequence. The three variants disagree about which
          patients are septic at $\kappa = \pcKappaVsBA$ (A vs B), so they
          define different cohorts, different prevalences and different
          denominators for every clinical claim downstream. Discrimination is
          simply insensitive to that.
    \item The split result is a null with power behind it: both arms are
          measured on the full MIMIC-IV test cohort, and it contradicts a
          widely held expectation (Section~\ref{sec:h4_discussion}). The
          transport row is the opposite case: a substantial point estimate
          with weak support. It rests on \pcExtNEventsB{} events, because the
          Sepsis-3 anchor is recoverable in only \pcExtMicroCoverage\% of
          eICU-CRD stays (Section~\ref{sec:external_coverage}), so its interval
          [\pcHFiveCiLo, \pcHFiveCiHi] is wide enough to contain both zero and
          a degradation twice the size of the label effect. It ranks second on
          the point estimate; it is not established at the confidence the other
          three rows carry.
\end{itemize}

What survives is therefore a sharper claim than the study's premise, pointing
in a different direction. Analyst decisions vary widely in how much they move
discrimination, and the two the literature scrutinises most, label reading
and split design, move it least. The remaining factor, which this framing
treats as a validation step rather than a choice, is the population the model
is asked to serve: its point estimate suggests it may matter more, but the
external analysis here is too imprecise to establish its contribution relative
to the others. Either way, the negative half of that result could not have been
obtained by fixing three factors and varying one.

\textbf{Of the three pre-registered hypotheses that admitted a statistical
test, H1 and H4 are not confirmed, while H6 supports one-sided non-inferiority
but not two-sided equivalence.} For H1 and H4 multiplicity control is not what
does the work. H1 and H4 return adjusted
p-values of \pcHOnePHolm{} and \pcHFourPHolm: each would have failed at any
correction, and each fails on its uncorrected value too. What overturns them is
quantifying uncertainty at all, and the estimate landing on the opposite side of
zero from the one predicted.

H6 returns \pcHSixPHolm{} as well, and it is a different quantity again. The
test is run against the upper side of the pre-registered equivalence bound,
$H_0\!: \theta \leq 0.02$ against $\theta > 0.02$, so its $p$-value asks
whether the boosted trees beat the hazard model by more than the margin. The
estimate lies far below the margin rather than above it, which is why the
adjusted value is \pcHSixPHolm: the test has nothing to reject, and a large
value here is therefore uninformative rather than a negative result. It is also
not what supports the non-inferiority conclusion. That conclusion comes from
the interval, whose upper limit \pcHSixCiHi{} lies below the margin
(Section~\ref{sec:h6}); the $p$-value neither establishes it nor contradicts
it.

H5's row is a different object and is worth separating. The pre-registered
hypothesis is not evaluable, so nothing about it is being tested here; what the
row reports is the substituted AUROC contrast. That contrast is inconclusive.
Its estimate falls on the predicted side of zero (\pcHFiveEst, in the direction
of external degradation) and its uncorrected p-value is the only one in the
family below 0.10, but its interval covers zero and its adjusted p-value is
\pcHFivePHolm. The external estimate rests on \pcExtNEventsB{} events
(Section~\ref{sec:external_interpretability}), and its interval is too wide to
distinguish no degradation from a substantial degradation, although the point
estimate is in the predicted direction. A
better-powered external cohort would have resolved that substituted contrast;
it would not have made the pre-registered H5 evaluable, because its
proportional Utility Score contrast remains undefined.

The pattern is worth stating plainly before the individual verdicts. H1 and H4
fail \emph{directionally}: the label does not out-vary the model, and the random
split is not shown to flatter the model, its interval covering zero while
excluding the pre-registered 0.02 threshold. H6 supports one-sided non-inferiority through
interval inversion: the upper confidence limit lies below the $+0.02$ margin,
although the implemented $p$-value does not establish that conclusion.
Two-sided equivalence is not established because the lower confidence limit
extends beyond $-0.02$. H2 is descriptive, and H3 and
H5 were not evaluable as pre-registered. The substituted contrast standing in
for H5 is inconclusive: its interval is wide and covers zero, so it is
compatible with no effect, though its point estimate is in the predicted
direction. A pre-registered design that returns this set of outcomes is doing
what pre-registration is for: the point estimates
under the original protocol, without intervals, would have supported three
confident claims that the data do not carry.

\subsection{H1: Label Dominance (Not Confirmed)}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUOrange!20, linecolor=ATUOrange]
    \textbf{H1 [C]:} Spread in AUROC across label variants $\geq$ spread across
    model classes.

    \textbf{Point estimates:} label spread $= \pcSpreadLabel$;
    model spread $= \pcSpreadModelClass$.

    \textbf{Contrast:} $\theta = \pcHOneEst$ (95\% CI
    \pcHOneCiLo--\pcHOneCiHi), Holm-adjusted $p = \pcHOnePHolm$.
    \textbf{H1 NOT CONFIRMED, and decisively so, in the direction opposite
    to the one hypothesised.}
\end{mdframed}

H1 fails, and not marginally. Model class produces an AUROC spread of
\pcSpreadModelClass{} against the label's \pcSpreadLabel{}, more than
tenfold, and the interval on the difference,
[\pcHOneCiLo, \pcHOneCiHi], excludes zero. \textbf{Which model class is fitted
matters far more for discrimination than which of three defensible Sepsis-3
readings is adopted.} The hypothesis anticipated the reverse.

This has to be read against what the two spreads contain. The model-class
spread runs from rule-based scores (qSOFA \pcAurocQsofaB, SIRS \pcAurocSirsB,
NEWS2 \pcAurocNewsB) through a static Cox model (\pcAurocCoxB) to the two
flexible estimators (primary \pcAurocPrimaryB, GBT \pcAurocGbtB). It is
therefore not a comparison of like with like: it is dominated by the gap between
a six-item bedside score and a fitted hazard model with 22 covariates, and a
gap of that size is not surprising. The label spread, by contrast, is measured
with the model held fixed and is genuinely small: the three variants disagree
about \emph{which} patients are septic far more than they disagree about how
predictable those patients are.

That distinction is what makes the negative result informative rather than
merely deflationary. Label choice moves the cohort substantially: variants A
and B agree on only \pcPctAgreeBA\% of stays ($\kappa = \pcKappaVsBA$) despite
near-identical prevalence (\pcPctOnsetsA\% against \pcPctOnsetsB\%). Yet all
three labels turn out to be about equally learnable. \textbf{The label
determines who is counted; it barely determines how well anyone can be
predicted.} A literature that fixes the label and compares only models is
therefore not mismeasuring discrimination, but it is silently fixing the
denominator of every clinical claim built on top of it.

One caveat applies to both spreads and is developed in
Section~\ref{sec:label_anchor_results}. Variants A, B and C are three readings
of one definition and all three fire on the same culture-plus-antibiotic
anchor. Sepsis-3 makes onset a function of treatment timing, so moving the
antibiotic--culture windows \emph{must} move the onsets: part of the label
spread measured here is guaranteed by the construction of the definition rather
than discovered in the data. Whether a label owing nothing to treatment timing
behaves differently cannot be answered inside this family, and is taken up
separately.

\subsection{H2: Coefficient Instability (Met, Descriptive)}
\label{sec:h2}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUSand!30, linecolor=ATUGreen]
    \textbf{H2 [C]:} At least one feature changes sign or magnitude
    $\geq 2\times$ across label variants.

    \textbf{Result:} of the \pcHTwoNTerms{} covariates shared by all three
    variants, \pcHTwoNSign{} reverse sign, \pcHTwoNMag{} change magnitude by
    $\geq 2\times$, and \pcHTwoEst{} do at least one of the two.
    \textbf{H2's criterion is met (descriptive; no test statistic).}

    \textbf{Multiplicity:} H2 reserves a slot in the confirmatory family of six
    but contributes no p-value, which makes the Holm correction on the
    remaining hypotheses stricter rather than weaker.
\end{mdframed}

H2 is a stability criterion, not a contrast between two estimable quantities,
so no p-value is defined for it. The count above is the pre-registered answer
and is reported unmodified. Its magnitude is conditional on the fit being
identified: a penalised specification with a unique maximum is a precondition
for the question ``does this coefficient change sign across labels?'' to have
an answer at all, and Section~\ref{sec:primary_model_spec} states the penalty
used.

The criterion has a weakness that its own form conceals, and which is worth
making explicit before the composition of the list is read. A threshold rule
applied to point estimates cannot separate a coefficient that genuinely takes
different values under different labels from one estimated so imprecisely that
a doubling falls within its noise. For a covariate whose standard error is of
the order of its estimate, the second is close to certain and says nothing
about label sensitivity. The count of \pcHTwoEst{} is therefore an
\emph{upper bound} on the instability present, and how loose a bound was, until
now, unmeasured.

Amendment 7)}
\label{sec:h2_uncertainty}

The bound above can now be tightened. A cluster-robust covariance for the
primary model, clustered on stay, is available
(Section~\ref{sec:primary_model_spec}), so each flagged covariate carries a
standard error under each label. For the two variants at the extremes of a
covariate's estimated range, the difference between them can be referred to the
pooled standard error of those two estimates. Table~\ref{tab:h2_uncertainty}
reports that ratio.

Two things follow, and they should be kept apart.

First, \textbf{H2's count is the pre-registered answer and stands}. The
criterion was fixed before any modelling, it is met, and
\pcHTwoEst{} of \pcHTwoNTerms{} covariates satisfy it. This diagnostic is
post-hoc, is disclosed as Protocol Amendment 7, and does not replace that
count. Restating the headline as the number surviving the diagnostic would
substitute a post-hoc estimand for a pre-registered one, which is precisely the
practice this thesis objects to elsewhere; the fact that the substitution would
be flattering here is a reason for more caution, not less.

Second, \textbf{the flagged set is not homogeneous, and the inhomogeneity has a
pattern}. The covariates whose movement survives comparison with their own
standard errors are \pcHTwoNRobustAction{} of \pcHTwoNRobust{} action-derived,
with ratios from \pcHTwoZMinRobust{} to \pcHTwoZMax, and all
\pcHTwoNRobust{} are indicators of whether a test was ordered. Those that do
not clear the threshold include both physiological covariates in the flagged
set and, equally, \emph{both sign reversals}: bilirubin and vasopressor
exposure carry the two smallest ratios in the table. The direction changes that
a bare count makes most striking are therefore the two the diagnostic supports
least, and no claim in this thesis rests on either of them individually. The
reading is that the label-sensitivity H2 detects is real but narrower than the
raw count suggests, and that it sits where the previous subsection and the
ablation both locate it: in the features that record what a clinician did
rather than what the patient's body was doing.

That convergence is worth stating carefully, because it is easy to overstate.
It is not independent corroboration. The ablation's split by feature kind
(Section~\ref{sec:h2_action}), the ablation arm of
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} and this diagnostic are all computed from the same
three fitted models and share the same flagged set. They are three readings of
one body of evidence, and they agree because they are looking at the same
thing from different angles, not because three separate experiments returned
the same answer.

\subsubsection{The instability is concentrated in the action-derived features}
\label{sec:h2_action}

The composition of this list matters more than its length, and it is not
arbitrary. The covariates fall into two kinds. \textbf{Action-derived}
features record clinician behaviour: vasopressor exposure, which is a
treatment, and the per-test order indicators, which record a decision to look.
\textbf{Physiological} features record the patient: vital signs, laboratory
values and their rolling slopes. Splitting the instability by kind separates
them sharply.

Action-derived covariates are a minority of the specification and a majority of
the unstable terms. The reading this licenses is more specific, and more useful,
than ``the model is unstable across labels'': what is unstable is the part of
the model that encodes clinician behaviour, and the part that encodes
physiology is comparatively stable.

That distinction matters because it is the same mechanism the thesis identifies
in the label, appearing now on the other side of the equation. Changing the
label variant changes which treatment episodes count as sepsis. Features built
from treatment and from measurement decisions are therefore re-anchored by a
label change in a way that a heart rate is not. The sign reversal on \emph{any
vasopressor} is the sharpest case: a published association between vasopressor
exposure and sepsis hazard, estimated from a model of this kind, cannot be
interpreted without knowing the exact label operationalisation, and the
same caution does not attach with equal force to the physiological terms.

Two limits on this reading should be stated. With \pcHTwoNTerms{} covariates in
total the counts are small, and the difference between the two rates is not
established at any conventional level of confidence; H2 carries no test
statistic (Section~\ref{sec:h2}) and the split inherits that limitation. And a
concentration of instability is not by itself evidence that the model
\emph{depends} on these features for its performance. Section~\ref{sec:ablation}
addresses the second point directly by removing them and refitting.

The instability is therefore real but narrower than a count alone would suggest:
\pcHTwoEst{} of \pcHTwoNTerms{} covariates, most by magnitude rather than
direction, and concentrated in features that are not measurements of the
patient. That supports Lauritsen et al.'s \cite{lauritsen2021framing}
framing-instability concern for specific effect estimates, and does not support
a claim that the fitted model is unstable as a whole.

\subsection{H3: Treatment Leakage, Definitional and Empirical}
\label{sec:h3}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUPurple!10, linecolor=ATUPurple]
    \textbf{H3 [C]:} Restricting to pre-treatment hours reduces AUROC by
    $\geq 0.03$.

    \textbf{Definitional result:} \textbf{\pcPreTreatOnsetsVarB{} sepsis onset
    events} in \pcPreTreatPersonHoursVarB{} pre-treatment person-hours. AUROC is
    undefined, so the pre-registered contrast cannot be formed. This zero is
    \emph{proved} below (Proposition~\ref{prop:h3}) to follow from the onset
    rule used by variants A--C; it is a statement about the implementation, not
    a measurement.

    \textbf{Empirical result (Protocol Amendment 4):} relabelling the same
    stays with the standard $\min(t_{\text{susp}}, t_{\text{SOFA}})$ onset
    (Variant F) moves \pcFNMovedEarlier{} of \pcFNOnsets{} onsets earlier
    (\pcFPctMovedEarlier\%, median \pcFMedianShiftH\,h) and places
    \textbf{\pcPreTreatOnsetsVarF{} onsets before treatment}, identified at
    \pcPreTreatAurocPrimaryF{} AUROC (\pcPreTreatAurocGbtF{} GBT).
    \textbf{H3 is not evaluable as pre-registered; the definitional and
    empirical statements replace it.}

    \textbf{Multiplicity:} like H2, H3 reserves a slot in the confirmatory
    family without contributing a p-value. Variant F's contrast against B is
    exploratory (family E3).
\end{mdframed}

\paragraph{The definitional half.}
When only hours before the first antibiotic or culture are considered, there
are zero sepsis onset events for variants A--C and E. This is not a small drop
in performance, and it is also not a finding. It is entailed by how the onset
timestamp is assigned:

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUSand!20, linecolor=ATUGreen]
    \begin{proposition}\label{prop:h3}
    Let $t_{\mathrm{anchor}}$ be the earlier of the first qualifying antibiotic
    order and the first culture draw of an admission, and let
    $t_{\mathrm{onset}}$ be the onset assigned by variants A, B, C or E. Then
    $t_{\mathrm{onset}} \geq t_{\mathrm{anchor}}$ for every labelled stay, so the
    window $[\,\cdot\,,\, t_{\mathrm{anchor}})$ contains no onset.
    \end{proposition}

    \emph{Proof.} Under E, $t_{\mathrm{onset}} = t_{\mathrm{susp}}$, and
    $t_{\mathrm{susp}}$ is defined as the \emph{later} element of a qualifying
    antibiotic-culture pair; both elements are at or after
    $t_{\mathrm{anchor}}$, so $t_{\mathrm{susp}} \geq t_{\mathrm{anchor}}$.
    Under A, B and C the SOFA criterion is a \emph{membership} gate: a stay is
    labelled only if the $\geq 2$-point rise occurs somewhere in the evaluation
    window, but the timestamp written is still $t_{\mathrm{susp}}$
    (Section~\ref{sec:alt_labels}). The inequality therefore carries over
    unchanged. $\square$
\end{mdframed}

\noindent The exact one-sided 95\% upper bound on the pre-treatment onset rate
reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}'s inference tables is arithmetically
correct but informationally empty: it bounds a quantity the definition fixes at
zero. It is retained only to make that point explicit.

\paragraph{The empirical half.}
The standard operationalisation does not have this property. Seymour et al.\ \cite{seymour2016assessment} and the PhysioNet/CinC 2019 Challenge
\cite{reyna2020utility} take
\[
    t_{\mathrm{onset}} = \min\bigl(t_{\mathrm{susp}},\, t_{\mathrm{SOFA}}\bigr),
\]
where $t_{\mathrm{SOFA}}$ is the first hour in the evaluation window at which
the organ-dysfunction rise is already present. When the SOFA rise is detected
before the clinician acts, the onset precedes the anchor and
Proposition~\ref{prop:h3} does not apply. Variant F implements exactly this and
nothing else: identical suspicion windows, identical SOFA criterion, therefore
\textbf{the same septic stays as Variant B}, all \pcFNOnsets{} of them,
with only the timestamp moved.

Under Variant F, \pcFNMovedEarlier{} onsets (\pcFPctMovedEarlier\%) move
earlier, by a median of \pcFMedianShiftH\,hours, and
\pcPreTreatOnsetsVarF{} of them fall in the pre-treatment window of the temporal
test set, where variants A--C record none. A model identifies those hours at
\pcPreTreatAurocPrimaryF{} AUROC. \textbf{Sepsis-3 onsets do precede clinical
action; the conjunction-timing rule used for the pre-registered variants is what
prevents any from being recorded.}

\paragraph{What the two halves together license, and what they do not.}
Neither half supports the absolute proposition that no patient develops sepsis
before clinicians begin treatment. The definitional half cannot support it,
because it is a statement about an onset rule rather than about patients; the
empirical half contradicts it. External evidence contradicts it too: the Epic
Sepsis Model v2 evaluation \cite{wong2026epic} scores against a composite of
clinician recognition and reports encounter-level AUROC of 0.80--0.90 with
median lead times of 1.4--7.1 hours ahead of that recognition. Prediction ahead
of clinical action is demonstrably possible.

What the two halves do license is narrower and more consequential. Sepsis-3
\emph{membership} is constituted by clinical action (no antibiotic-culture
pair, no label, under any timing rule), so Kamran et al.'s
\cite{kamran2024evaluation} argument holds at the level of \emph{which} patients
can ever be labelled. And a conjunction-based onset rule, in common with much of
the MIMIC-IV literature, discards the timing branch that makes a pre-treatment
prediction task definable at all. A result reported on such a label is measuring
discrimination around the clinician's decision, within a window that decision
opened.
Section~\ref{sec:pretreatment_label} sets Variant F beside the
anchor-independent deterioration label and reports both.

\subsection{H4: Split Optimism (Not Confirmed)}
\label{sec:h4_discussion}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUOrange!20, linecolor=ATUOrange]
    \textbf{H4 [C]:} Temporal-split AUROC is at least 0.02 lower than
    random-split AUROC.

    \textbf{Result:} primary model, random AUROC \pcAurocRandomPrimaryB{}
    against temporal \pcAurocPrimaryB. Difference $= \pcHFourEst$ (95\% CI
    \pcHFourCiLo--\pcHFourCiHi): the temporal split is the \emph{higher} of
    the two. Test against the pre-registered threshold $\theta_0 = 0.02$:
    Holm-adjusted $p = \pcHFourPHolm$. \textbf{H4 NOT CONFIRMED.}
\end{mdframed}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{llrr}
        \toprule

H4 anticipated that random splitting would increase AUROC by at least 0.02
relative to temporal splitting. The results do not support that pre-registered
magnitude: the point estimate favours the temporal split, while the interval
includes zero and excludes $+0.02$. The direction is
consistent across all four models fitted under both splits: each scores
\emph{lower} on the random split, by \pcSplitDeltaMinAbs{} to \pcSplitDeltaMaxAbs. The interval on the primary
model's difference, [\pcHFourCiLo, \pcHFourCiHi], is centred below zero and
excludes the 0.02 threshold the hypothesis required.

The explanation is that the two splits differ in more than optimism. The
temporal test set is the 2017--2019 admission cohort, whereas the random test
set is a 25\% patient-level sample spanning all years. The random arm therefore
trains on \emph{less} data from any given era and tests on a more
heterogeneous mixture, and that cost evidently outweighs whatever leakage
advantage it gains. Guo et al.'s \cite{guo2022temporal} concern is not refuted
by this (their argument is about optimism from temporal leakage, which is
real), but on this cohort it is not the dominant term, and a study choosing
between the two designs cannot assume the random split is the more permissive
one.

There is a second reason not to read too much into the size of this contrast.
The two test sets are disjoint samples of different composition, so the
difference confounds split design with cohort composition; it is not the clean
within-sample optimism estimate the hypothesis presumed. This is recorded in
the limitations (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{H5: Metric Divergence (Not Evaluable as Pre-Registered)}
\label{sec:h5}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUOrange!20, linecolor=ATUOrange]
    \textbf{H5 [C]:} Utility Score declines proportionally more than AUROC from
    internal to external validation.

    \textbf{Testable arm:} AUROC degradation internal $\to$ external (primary,
    Variant B) $= \pcHFiveEst$ (95\% CI \pcHFiveCiLo--\pcHFiveCiHi); internal
    \pcAurocPrimaryB, eICU-CRD \pcExtAurocPrimaryB{} on \pcExtNEventsB{}
    external events. Holm-adjusted $p = \pcHFivePHolm$.
    \textbf{The point estimate falls, but the interval does not exclude no
    change.}

    \textbf{Non-testable arm:} the internal Utility Score is
    \pcUtilityPrimaryB{} at the default cut-off and \pcUtilityMaxPrimaryB{} at
    its best threshold, so a proportional decline cannot be formed.
    \textbf{H5 AS PRE-REGISTERED IS NOT EVALUABLE; the substituted AUROC
    contrast is not supported.}
\end{mdframed}

The pre-registered hypothesis cannot be evaluated at all, and the contrast that
occupies its place is inconclusive. The two limitations are of different kinds
and should be separated.

\paragraph{The estimand tested is a substitute, and the adjusted $p$-value
belongs to it.} H5 as pre-registered compares two \emph{proportional} declines,
and that contrast cannot be formed here: a proportional decline requires a
baseline to be proportional to, and the internal Utility Score under the primary
label is \pcUtilityPrimaryB{} at the default cut-off and \pcUtilityMaxPrimaryB{}
at its best threshold. What occupies H5's slot in the confirmatory family is
therefore the AUROC limb alone (internal minus external AUROC, against
$\theta_0 = 0$), and the Holm-adjusted value of \pcHFivePHolm{} reported above
applies to that substituted contrast, not to the metric-divergence estimand the
protocol specified. The substitution, its motivation and the constraints placed
on it are recorded in \S13.6 of \texttt{00\_preregistration.md} alongside the
two hypotheses that are degenerate outright. It should be read with one caution
stated in the same place: this substituted contrast produces the smallest raw
$p$-value in the family, and it is still inconclusive.

The substituted AUROC contrast is inconclusive: its interval is wide and
covers zero, though its point estimate is in the predicted direction. External
discrimination on eICU-CRD is \pcExtAurocPrimaryB{} against \pcAurocPrimaryB{}
internally, a degradation of \pcHFiveEst{}: the sign H5 predicted, and
roughly three-quarters of the 0.085 anticipated from Moor et al.\
\cite{moor2023review}. But the interval [\pcHFiveCiLo, \pcHFiveCiHi] covers
zero, so the estimate is equally consistent with no loss and with a loss twice
as large. This is the only tested contrast in the family whose uncorrected
p-value falls below 0.10, and the only one a better-powered external cohort
could have resolved.

The precision is bounded by documentation coverage, not by cohort size.
eICU-CRD contains \pcExtNCohortStays{} unit stays, but the Sepsis-3 conjunction
is recoverable in \pcExtMicroCoverage\% of them, so the analysis rests on
\pcExtNEventsB{} events against an interpretability floor of \pcExtMinEvents{}
(Section~\ref{sec:external_interpretability}). Person-hours from the remaining
stays would enlarge the denominator without contributing a single observable
onset.

Two further observations point the same way, and are reported for that reason
rather than as evidence that settles the question. First,
the effect is ordered across labels exactly as the internal results are: the
Narrow label loses most (\pcAurocPrimaryA{} to \pcExtAurocPrimaryA) and the
Liberal label least (\pcAurocPrimaryC{} to \pcExtAurocPrimaryC). Neither of
those two contrasts carries an interval, and an ordering of three noisy
estimates is not itself evidence of an underlying order, so this is a
consistency observation and not a second test. Second, the antibiotic-only arm (a different target,
but one measured on \pcExtAbxNEventsB{} events and therefore well clear of the
floor) shows a degradation of the same sign and larger magnitude
(\pcAurocPrimaryB{} to \pcExtAbxAurocPrimaryB) with the same ordering across
labels. Two arms differing by two orders of magnitude in event count agree on
sign, ordering and approximate size.

The honest verdict is therefore neither ``discrimination transports'' nor
``discrimination collapses''. The point estimates consistently suggest external
degradation, and the study establishes neither its existence nor its magnitude
on the Sepsis-3 arm.

The Utility Score arm cannot be formed for a different and more consequential reason: the
margin whose collapse Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2025srpm} documented, a median internal Utility Score of $+0.381$ falling to $-0.164$, does not
exist here to begin with. The best internal score any configuration reaches
under the primary label is \pcUtilityMaxGbtB, against internal AUROC in the high
0.7s. A ratio of proportional declines is undefined against a baseline this
close to zero, and the \pcExtNEventsB{} external events are in any case too few
to estimate an external Utility Score reliably.

The finding that replaces H5 is therefore not ``utility degrades faster than
AUROC''. It is something the internal results establish on their own, without
needing the external arm at all: \textbf{no evaluated operating point combines
usable clinical value with a tolerable alert burden, at any
threshold, on the cohort where the label is fully observed}. The broader
concern Wang et al.\ raised, that discrimination and clinical-utility measures
can give materially different assessments of the same models, is reproduced
internally here, though not as a test of the pre-registered H5 contrast; their
specific mechanism, external collapse of a healthy internal utility, is not
what happens here. What the
external arm adds is a qualification in their favour on the other metric: AUROC
does appear to decline on transport, so the stability they observed in it
(0.811 to 0.783) is not reproduced as stability here either.

There is a methodological point in this that outlives the particular numbers.
External validation is conventionally treated as a test of the model, and the
label is assumed to travel with it. It does not. Sepsis-3 is defined on
clinician actions and on the documentation of those actions, so transporting it
requires the destination database to record the same actions in the same
retrievable form. Where it does not, the failure presents as an implausibly low
event rate rather than as an error, and an AUROC computed on the survivors is
still a well-formed number, which is precisely what makes it dangerous. The
event-rate reconciliation in Table~\ref{tab:external_rates} exists so that this
check is performed and reported rather than assumed.

\subsection{H6: Model-Class Null (Not Rejected; Non-Inferiority Established)}
\label{sec:h6}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUSand!30, linecolor=ATUGreen]
    \textbf{H6 [C]:} $|\Delta_\text{AUROC}(\text{GBT} - \text{primary})| \leq 0.02$.

    \textbf{Result:} $\Delta = \pcHSixEst$ (GBT \pcAurocGbtB, primary
    \pcAurocPrimaryB), 95\% CI \pcHSixCiLo--\pcHSixCiHi. The \emph{upper} limit
    lies inside the bound $\theta_0 = 0.02$; the lower limit does not.
    \textbf{H6 NOT REJECTED. What this establishes is non-inferiority of the
    interpretable model (the boosted trees are not better by more than the
    margin) and not two-sided equivalence.}
\end{mdframed}

This is the strongest positive result in the study, and it is worth stating
exactly what it is and is not.

An interpretable multinomial hazard model with 29 terms per outcome
(22 covariates, a six-column natural-spline baseline in elapsed hours, and an
intercept) is not outperformed by gradient-boosted
trees on discrimination under the primary label: \pcAurocPrimaryB{} against
\pcAurocGbtB. Both models are scored on the same person-hours, so the contrast
is paired and the interval is correspondingly tight. The ordering is the same
under the other two labels (Table~\ref{tab:confirmatory}).

\paragraph{What the interval supports, and what it does not.} The pre-registered
statement is two-sided, $|\theta| \leq 0.02$, but the test performed against it
is one-sided: the bootstrap $p$-value is computed against
$H_0\!: \theta \leq 0.02$, which asks whether the boosted trees beat the
hazard model by more than the margin. It does not, so that test has nothing to
reject and its $p$-value is \pcHSixPHolm.

The non-inferiority conclusion rests on the interval rather than on that
$p$-value, and the distinction matters because failing to reject
$\theta \leq 0.02$ could never establish $\theta \leq 0.02$. What does
establish it is inversion: the interval's upper limit is \pcHSixCiHi, which
lies below the margin, so every value of $\theta$ the data are compatible with
at this confidence is smaller than $0.02$. That is the standard interval form
of a non-inferiority conclusion, and it is the form this thesis claims. Establishing two-sided equivalence would require the second of the
two one-sided tests as well, and the data do not deliver it: the lower limit
\pcHSixCiLo{} falls outside $-0.02$, so a difference larger than the margin in
the \emph{interpretable} model's favour remains consistent with these data. The
defensible reading is therefore asymmetric: the boosted trees buy nothing here,
but the two estimators have not been shown to be interchangeable, and a claim of
strict equivalence would overstate the evidence.

One further property of the interval is worth reading off, because it is
stronger than non-inferiority and weaker than it might appear. The interval
lies entirely below zero, so these data are not consistent with the two
estimators discriminating equally well: on the primary label the interpretable
model is ahead, not merely not behind. The size of that lead is what keeps the
statement modest. It is \pcHSixEst{} of AUROC, of the same order as the label
spread and far smaller than the model-class range, and the utility comparison
below shows it buys nothing at any operating point. The claim this licenses is that
the accuracy case for the opaque model fails, not that the transparent one
should be preferred on accuracy grounds; those are different claims and only
the first is needed. A methodological caution on the comparator's fitting
procedure, which bears on the direction of this result, is stated in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The asymmetry does not weaken the methodological conclusion, because the
conclusion only ever needed one side of it. The argument for preferring an
opaque model over a transparent one is an argument that the opaque model
performs better; the interval excludes that in the only direction it is made.

The methodological consequence is direct. On tabular ICU time-series of this
kind, the accuracy argument for preferring an opaque model over a transparent
one is not supported here. Whatever a boosted ensemble is extracting from these
features, a pooled logistic hazard model with the same features and an
appropriately flexible baseline extracts as well, and the latter yields
per-covariate coefficients, a calibration slope that can be inspected, and a
functional form a clinician can be shown.

The clinical-utility comparison tells the same story from the other side, and
matters more. At the default cut-off the primary model appears far ahead
(\pcUtilityPrimaryB{} against \pcUtilityGbtB{}) purely because it never crosses
0.5 and GBT does; that contrast measures calibration, not clinical value. Once
each model is evaluated at its own best threshold the two are close and GBT is
marginally ahead, \pcUtilityMaxGbtB{} against \pcUtilityMaxPrimaryB{}. The two
metrics therefore order the models oppositely, and both differences are too
small to act on: whichever way the comparison is run, neither model is worth
choosing over the other on clinical value. Both
maxima sit a few per cent above the no-alert strategy, which is the more
important observation: \textbf{the model-class choice is nearly free, because
neither class delivers clinical value worth choosing between.}

\label{sec:register}
%===========================================================================

Table~\ref{tab:register} records the status of every analysis reported in this
chapter and the multiplicity correction applied to it. Its confirmatory and
exploratory rows are transcribed from
\texttt{12\_analysis\_\allowbreak register\allowbreak .parquet}, which the
inference stage writes from the same objects it corrects, so the status and
correction attached to a hypothesis are the ones the code applied rather than
the ones this chapter claims for it. Two rows have no counterpart in that
file and are marked accordingly: the antibiotic-only external arm and the
by-kind stability split are scored in stages 07 and 08 and enter no corrected
family, so the inference stage never sees them. They are recorded here because
a register that omitted them would understate how much of this chapter is
exploratory.
