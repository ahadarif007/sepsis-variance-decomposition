\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

\vspace*{-3\baselineskip}

Over ninety AI models have been built to predict sepsis in real time, yet they
fail in practice: Wang et al.\ (2025) report a clinical Utility Score falling
from $+0.381$ internally to $-0.164$ externally while AUROC barely moves. This
thesis asks whether that failure belongs to the model, to the sepsis label, or
to the analyst's discretionary choices.

Using MIMIC-IV v3.1 (\pcNStays{} adult ICU stays) with external validation on
eICU-CRD v2.0, three pre-registered Sepsis-3 label variants are crossed with
six model specifications (a discrete-time hazard model, gradient-boosted trees,
a static Cox baseline and three bedside scores), two split designs and two
evaluation metrics, under
stay-level cluster-bootstrap intervals with Holm and Benjamini--Hochberg
correction. The models estimate the cause-specific hazard of onset in the
current hour, so every AUROC reported here scores discrimination of the onset
hour among at-risk hours rather than anticipation of onset over a future
horizon. Because all three variants share one culture-plus-antibiotic
anchor, the conjunction is additionally split into its limbs (including a
label built from physiology alone, with no treatment timestamp) and re-timed
under the standard $\min(t_{\text{susp}}, t_{\text{SOFA}})$ onset rule.

The results invert the study's premise. Model class dominates the
decomposition, moving AUROC by \pcSpreadModelClass{} against the label's
\pcSpreadLabel{} on an interval excluding zero. Of the three pre-registered
hypotheses admitting formal statistical evaluation, H1 and H4 are not confirmed
and both fail in the direction opposite to the one predicted, while H6 supports
one-sided non-inferiority but not two-sided equivalence. H2 is evaluated
descriptively, H3 and H5 are not evaluable as pre-registered, and the amended
contrast standing in for H5 is inconclusive. An interpretable hazard model is not outperformed by gradient-boosted trees,
leading them by \pcHSixAbsEst{} AUROC, and the results do not support the
pre-registered claim that random splitting inflates AUROC by at least 0.02
relative to temporal splitting. Every external point estimate is lower than its internal
counterpart, but the Sepsis-3 anchor is recoverable in only
\pcExtMicroCoverage\% of eICU-CRD stays and the tested contrast's interval
covers zero, so neither degradation nor its magnitude is established for the
Sepsis-3 target.

The label's consequence lies elsewhere. Under the primary label no model
exceeds a Utility Score of \pcUtilityMaxGbtB{} at any threshold, or
\pcUtilityMaxGbtC{} under any pre-registered variant, on a scale bounded by 1;
the two
narrowest readings agree at only $\kappa = \pcKappaVsBA{}$ on which patients are
septic,
so the label fixes the cohort and the denominator of every clinical quantity
computed on it. And the pre-treatment window contains zero Sepsis-3 events,
proved here to follow from the conjunction-based onset rule rather than
discovered in the data. Re-timing the \emph{same} labelled stays by the
standard rule places \pcPreTreatOnsetsVarF{} onsets before treatment begins;
the anchor-independent label places \pcPreTreatOnsetsVarD{} there.

Improving discrimination alone does not resolve the membership and timing
limitations of a treatment-anchored label: its \emph{membership} is
constituted by clinical actions, and its onset \emph{timing} decides whether a
prediction task defined before those actions can be posed at all.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sepsis; MIMIC-IV; discrete-time survival analysis;
label definition; variance decomposition; clinical utility; TRIPOD+AI.
