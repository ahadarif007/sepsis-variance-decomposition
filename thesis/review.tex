\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:review}

This chapter reviews the statistical and machine-learning literature on sepsis
prediction in the intensive care unit, traces the methodological lineage that
informs the present work, and formalises three research gaps that the thesis
addresses. The review is structured thematically: Section~\ref{sec:sepsis_def}
introduces the clinical definition; Section~\ref{sec:stat_onset} covers
statistical onset-prediction models; Section~\ref{sec:prog} surveys prognostic
nomograms and trajectory studies on MIMIC-IV;
Section~\ref{sec:ml_onset} reviews the machine-learning sepsis prediction
landscape; Section~\ref{sec:landmark_miss} discusses landmarking and
informative missingness; Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} reviews benchmarks and
systematic reviews; and Section~\ref{sec:gap_summary} synthesises the gaps.

\section{The Sepsis-3 Definition and Its Operationalisation}
\label{sec:sepsis_def}

The Sepsis-3 consensus \cite{singer2016sepsis3} defines sepsis as
life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to
infection, operationalised as suspected infection accompanied by an acute
rise of two or more points in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)
score \cite{vincent1996sofa}. Seymour \emph{et al.}
\cite{seymour2016criteria} validated this operationalisation in large
observational cohorts. Two features of this definition are methodologically
consequential for prediction research. First, the label is \emph{retrospective}:
it depends on the clinician ordering cultures and antibiotics, so ``onset'' partly
reflects the moment of clinical recognition rather than the true biological
transition. Second, sepsis that is already present at ICU admission
(\emph{prevalent} sepsis) is not predictable by an early-warning system;
only \emph{incident} sepsis developing after admission is the
appropriate target, yet many studies do not distinguish between the two.

\section{Statistical Onset Prediction}
\label{sec:stat_onset}

The foundational statistical benchmark for real-time sepsis prediction is
TREWScore \cite{henry2015trewscore}, a Cox proportional-hazards model fitted on
routinely collected MIMIC-II vitals and laboratory values. TREWScore achieved an
area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.83 with a
median lead time of 28 hours before clinical recognition of septic shock. It
demonstrated that a small set of physiological variables, modelled with a
well-understood statistical framework, can anticipate clinical deterioration in
real time. However, TREWScore was developed on MIMIC-II, which has since been
superseded by two major database revisions. It has not been revisited on
MIMIC-IV with purely statistical, interpretable tools.

Liaw \emph{et al.} \cite{liaw2019mortality} proposed a longitudinal,
multivariable survival model (a complementary log-log formulation) that tracks
evolving mortality risk from repeatedly measured indicators, demonstrating the
value of updating covariate values over the course of a stay rather than relying
on a baseline snapshot. This
time-varying approach is directly relevant to the current work, which extends
the idea from mortality modelling to onset prediction. Kasal \emph{et al.}
\cite{kasal2004cox} compared a standard Cox model against a flexible survival
model in severe sepsis and found that hazards are frequently
\emph{non-proportional}---a methodological caution that has been largely ignored
by subsequent MIMIC-IV studies. In the present review, no MIMIC-IV Cox study was
found that reports testing the proportional-hazards assumption before
interpreting hazard ratios, creating a gap in the validity of their reported
effect sizes (\textbf{G2}).

\section{Prognostic Modelling on MIMIC-IV}
\label{sec:prog}

A well-established MIMIC-IV pattern couples LASSO variable selection with
multivariable logistic regression to predict in-hospital mortality
\emph{after} a sepsis diagnosis has been made, typically presented as a
nomogram \cite{yuan2023nomogram}. These studies are often methodologically
mature: they use penalised regression to guard against overfitting, report
calibration curves and decision-curve analysis, and in several cases include
external validation on the eICU Collaborative Research Database
\cite{pollard2018eicu}. However, they address a fundamentally different clinical
question---prognosis after diagnosis---rather than the early detection of
onset itself.

Group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM) of SOFA scores represents a second
strand of MIMIC-IV research. Yang \emph{et al.} \cite{yang2022gbtm} fitted
latent-class growth models to 72-hour SOFA trajectories and linked trajectory
membership to mortality via Cox regression. Their approach captures the
\emph{shape} of organ-dysfunction evolution, not merely its endpoint, and
demonstrated a strong dose-response relationship between trajectory class and
mortality. This trajectory-modelling thread informs the present work: however,
Yang \emph{et al.} studied only mortality, and their analysis did not link
trajectory classes to incident sepsis onset, nor did they test whether the
trajectory shape carries predictive signal beyond its terminal SOFA value.

\section{Machine-Learning Approaches to Sepsis Prediction}
\label{sec:ml_onset}

The machine-learning literature on sepsis prediction is substantially larger
than the statistical literature and has been the subject of multiple systematic
reviews. Gradient-boosted trees, recurrent neural networks, and transformer
architectures have been applied to the PhysioNet/CinC 2019 Challenge data
\cite{reyna2020physionet} and to MIMIC-IV directly, often achieving high
internal discrimination. However, Fleuren \emph{et al.} \cite{fleuren2020ml}
conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of machine-learning sepsis
prediction models and reported large between-study heterogeneity in design,
inclusion criteria, and reported performance. Several observations from this
review are relevant:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Internal AUROCs are often high but difficult to compare across studies
  because of differing outcome definitions, cohort construction, and temporal
  alignment of predictions to outcomes.
  \item Feature engineering, particularly the use of temporal features and
  laboratory ordering patterns, varies widely across studies, and the
  contribution of these engineered features versus raw measurements is rarely
  isolated.
  \item External validation is infrequent, and when performed, performance
  drops substantially.
\end{itemize}

A central argument of the present thesis is that interpretable statistical
models retain specific advantages over black-box alternatives for clinical
deployment: they yield directly interpretable hazard ratios and odds ratios,
their assumptions can be formally tested and reported, and they can be
benchmarked transparently against established rule-based scores. When the
prediction ceiling is constrained by the labelling process rather than by
model complexity, interpretability becomes a net gain rather than a cost.

\section{Dynamic Landmarking and Informative Missingness}
\label{sec:landmark_miss}

Dynamic prediction by landmarking \cite{vanhouwelingen2007landmark} provides
a principled framework for continuously updated risk prediction from survival
data. The idea is simple but powerful: at each prediction time $s$ (the
``landmark''), the analysis is restricted to patients still at risk, their
current covariate values are used, and a short-horizon outcome is modelled.
The landmark supermodel formulation \cite{fries2023dynamiclm} extends this by
stacking multiple landmark datasets and fitting a single penalised model with
the landmark time $s$, its polynomial terms, and feature-by-$s$ interactions as
covariates. This yields one interpretable model that emits hourly-updating risk,
rather than requiring a separate model at each prediction hour. To the
author's knowledge, this approach has not previously been applied to
statistical Sepsis-3 onset prediction on MIMIC-IV.

A separate but complementary thread concerns informative missingness in
electronic health records. Groenwold \cite{groenwold2020missingness} formalised
``the curse of knowing'': in clinical data, the fact that a test was ordered
is itself informative, because clinicians order tests in response to suspicion.
Singh \emph{et al.} \cite{singh2021missingness} showed empirically that binary
``was this lab measured'' indicators can outperform the measured values
themselves as predictors of clinical outcomes. This phenomenon is particularly
relevant to sepsis prediction, where laboratory tests such as lactate,
creatinine, and the P/F ratio are ordered preferentially in sicker patients.
An early-warning model that ignores missingness patterns discards a
clinically meaningful signal.

\section{Benchmarks, Validation, and Systematic Reviews}
\label{sec:benchmark}

The PhysioNet/CinC 2019 Challenge \cite{reyna2020physionet} established a
standardised early-prediction benchmark with a clinical-utility metric that
rewards timely alarms and penalises both late detection and false alarms. This
utility score provides a more clinically meaningful evaluation than AUROC alone,
because it captures the temporal aspect of prediction: an alarm that fires six
hours before onset is more valuable than one that fires at the moment of onset,
and an alarm that fires on a non-septic patient imposes a real cost in
clinician attention.

Wang \emph{et al.} \cite{wang2025srpm} conducted the most comprehensive
methodological review of sepsis real-time prediction models to date, covering
91 studies. Their central finding is that the median AUROC falls from 0.886
under internal validation to 0.783 under full external validation. This
degradation is consistent across model types and underscores that the
internal-to-external gap, rather than raw internal discrimination, is the
field's central weakness. Critically, this validation gap is most pronounced
for onset-prediction models, precisely the task this thesis addresses.
External validation is common in the MIMIC-IV prognostic nomogram literature
but essentially absent from the onset-prediction and trajectory literature
(\textbf{G3}).

\section{Summary of Research Gaps}
\label{sec:gap_summary}

Synthesising the above, the three research gaps that structure this thesis are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{G1 (Onset versus prognosis).} MIMIC-IV statistical work
  overwhelmingly predicts mortality after diagnosis. Genuine pre-onset,
  real-time detection with purely statistical tools has not been revisited on
  MIMIC-IV since TREWScore's 2015 MIMIC-II study \cite{henry2015trewscore}.
  The dominant MIMIC-IV pattern (LASSO + nomogram) addresses prognosis, not
  onset \cite{yuan2023nomogram}; and the trajectory literature (GBTM) links
  SOFA shapes to mortality, not to incident onset \cite{yang2022gbtm}.

  \item \textbf{G2 (Untested proportional hazards).} Non-proportional hazards
  in sepsis have been documented \cite{kasal2004cox}, yet no reviewed MIMIC-IV
  Cox study reports testing the assumption. When the assumption fails and is
  not addressed, the reported hazard ratios are unreliable; the appropriate
  response is a time-varying reformulation, which this thesis implements.

  \item \textbf{G3 (Validation asymmetry).} External validation is common in
  the prognostic nomogram literature \cite{yuan2023nomogram} but largely absent
  from onset-prediction and trajectory studies. Wang \emph{et al.}
  \cite{wang2025srpm} showed that this is precisely where performance degrades
  most, making external validation a necessary step rather than a desirable
  extra.
\end{enumerate}

The contributions listed in Section~\ref{sec:contrib} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} address each gap directly. Collectively, the
gaps define a space for a purely statistical, interpretable, externally validated
real-time sepsis onset model on MIMIC-IV, which is the contribution of this
thesis.
