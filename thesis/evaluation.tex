\chapter{System Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter presents the experimental results of the pipeline, organised
around the three sub-questions of Section~\ref{sec:rq}. The dynamic landmark
supermodel (Analysis~2) is the central contribution and receives the most detailed
treatment. Internal MIMIC-IV results are presented first, followed by the
external eICU-CRD validation and a critical discussion of strengths,
limitations, and threats to validity.

%==========================================================================
\section{Temporal Foundation: Onset Distribution and the Landmark Design}
%==========================================================================

Before reporting model performance, two empirical facts about the data must be
established, as they shape every subsequent result.

\paragraph{Prevalent versus incident sepsis.}
Of the 65{,}241 stays in the cohort, 37.1\% satisfy the Sepsis-3 label on the
first-24h analysis matrix (Section~\ref{sec:outcome}); an exact hourly onset
time could then be located within the 72-hour panel for 32.0\% of all stays
(20{,}861 stays), which constitute the temporal cohort used from here on. Of
these temporally labelled cases, 34\% are already septic at ICU admission
(onset hour 0).
These prevalent cases are not predictable by any early-warning system.
Conditioning at the hour-six landmark yields \textbf{48{,}829 at-risk stays},
of which 4{,}750 (9.7\%) develop incident sepsis and 3{,}330 (6.8\%) develop
it within 12 hours. This is the correct, realistically imbalanced prediction
target.

\paragraph{Time-to-onset survival analysis.}
Figure~\ref{fig:km_cumulative} shows the Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence of
sepsis onset from the hour-six landmark, stratified by SIRS status. Patients
meeting $\ge 2$ SIRS criteria at the landmark reach sepsis substantially faster
(14.2\% versus 7.8\% by 24 hours after landmark; log-rank statistic 360,
$p < 10^{-16}$).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/09_km_cumulative_incidence.png}
    \caption{Cumulative incidence of sepsis onset from the hour-six landmark,
    stratified by SIRS status. Patients with $\ge 2$ SIRS criteria develop
    sepsis faster, confirming that inflammatory markers carry predictive signal
    for the incident-onset task.}
    \label{fig:km_cumulative}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Competing risks.}
Death and discharge compete with sepsis onset: a patient who dies
sepsis-free can never develop sepsis, so treating death as ordinary censoring
overstates the onset incidence. Figure~\ref{fig:competing_risks} shows the
Fine-Gray cumulative incidence functions, confirming that SIRS raises the
cumulative likelihood of onset even after accounting for the competing event
(subdistribution hazard ratio for SIRS: 1.49).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/09_competing_risks_cif.png}
    \caption{Cumulative incidence functions from the hour-six landmark
    accounting for death without sepsis as a competing risk, stratified by
    SIRS status. The solid lines show onset incidence; the dashed lines show
    the competing risk of death without sepsis.}
    \label{fig:competing_risks}
\end{figure}

\section{Proportional-Hazards Testing and Time-Varying Cox (SQ1, C2)}
%==========================================================================

A baseline Cox model estimates the hazard of onset from hour-six covariates.
Table~\ref{table:cox_baseline} reports the hazard ratios.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{l|r|c|c}
        \hline
        \textbf{Term} & \textbf{HR} & \textbf{95\% CI} & \textbf{$p$} \\
        \hline\hline
        Age           & 1.009 & [1.007, 1.011] & $< 10^{-16}$ \\
        Heart rate    & 1.011 & [1.009, 1.013] & $< 10^{-16}$ \\
        Resp.\ rate   & 1.021 & [1.019, 1.023] & $< 10^{-16}$ \\
        MAP           & 0.988 & [0.986, 0.990] & $< 10^{-16}$ \\
        SOFA total    & 0.762 & [0.748, 0.777] & $< 10^{-16}$ \\
        SIRS          & 1.150 & [1.104, 1.198] & $2.5 \times 10^{-11}$ \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Cox model for time to incident sepsis (hour-six landmark). SIRS
    and vital signs carry the hazard, while a single-timepoint SOFA value is
    \emph{inversely} associated with incident onset.}
    \label{table:cox_baseline}
\end{table}

A critical finding is that \textbf{SOFA at hour 6 is inversely associated}
with incident onset (HR 0.76): patients with high organ dysfunction at hour 6
but no evolving infection tend \emph{not} to cross the Sepsis-3 threshold
later. This counterintuitive result is clinically meaningful: it demonstrates
that severity scores detect established dysfunction, not impending infection.

The global Schoenfeld residual test \textbf{rejects proportional hazards}
($p < 10^{-16}$), confirming on MIMIC-IV the non-proportionality that Kasal
\emph{et al.} \cite{kasal2004cox} reported elsewhere (C2). In response, a
\textbf{time-varying Cox model} is fitted on the counting-process formulation,
updating covariates hour by hour. The time-varying model locates the hazard
in \emph{current heart rate} (HR 1.019 per bpm), while the single SOFA value
remains inversely associated (HR 0.80): the predictive signal lives in
\emph{physiological dynamics}, not in a severity snapshot.

\section{Temporal Structure: Trajectories and Autocorrelation}
%==========================================================================

The cross-sectional analysis treated stays as independent observations.
Within a patient, however, the hourly measurements form a genuine time series
with strong serial dependence. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectories_acf} shows the
mean heart-rate trajectories by sepsis status (left panel) and the
distribution of within-patient lag-1 autocorrelation of heart rate (right
panel).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/09_trajectories_acf.png}
    \caption{Left: mean hourly heart rate by sepsis status; septic patients
    run higher and diverge over time. Right: distribution of within-patient
    lag-1 autocorrelation of heart rate; the mean of 0.57 confirms strong
    hour-to-hour dependence that the static analysis discarded.}
    \label{fig:trajectories_acf}
\end{figure}

The mean within-patient lag-1 autocorrelation of heart rate is
\textbf{0.57}, confirming that hourly observations within a patient are
strongly dependent. This serial correlation is the statistical foundation
that sequence models (including the landmark supermodel) are built to exploit:
a model that conditions on the trajectory of recent measurements captures
information that a single-timepoint snapshot discards.

Table~\ref{table:bench} reports discrimination at the hour-six landmark for

This result answers SQ3 on internal data: \textbf{no single clinical score
is useful} for incident sepsis. SOFA is even \emph{inverse} (AUROC 0.36),

Sepsis-3. SIRS is the best single score (AUROC 0.59), consistent with its
sensitivity to inflammatory signals. A multivariable combination of hourly
measurements reaches AUROC 0.749, and adding six-hour trajectory slopes
raises it to 0.755 (DeLong $p = 3.5 \times 10^{-7}$), supporting SQ2: the
trajectory carries information a snapshot does not.

\section{Analysis 1: Informative Missingness and the Nomogram (C4)}
%==========================================================================

The full-feature elastic-net nomogram reaches AUROC \textbf{0.775}, a
statistically significant gain over the 0.755 model (DeLong $p < 10^{-16}$).
The most striking finding is \emph{what} carries the signal: the ten largest
coefficients are dominated by \textbf{missingness indicators}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{l|r|r}
        \hline
        \textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Coefficient} & \textbf{Odds Ratio} \\
        \hline\hline
        pf\_ratio\_msg (was P/F measured)  & 1.697 & 5.456 \\
        creatinine\_msg                    & 0.450 & 1.568 \\
        hr\_slope6\_msg                    & 0.391 & 1.479 \\
        female                             & $-0.388$ & 0.678 \\
        sofa\_total                        & $-0.372$ & 0.690 \\
        wbc\_msg                           & 0.306 & 1.358 \\
        temp\_c                            & 0.203 & 1.225 \\
        qsofa                              & 0.160 & 1.174 \\
        sirs                               & 0.105 & 1.110 \\
        gcs                                & 0.074 & 1.076 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Ten largest coefficients (original scale) in the Analysis-1 elastic-net
    model. Features ending in \_msg are binary ``was measured'' indicators.
    The act of ordering a P/F ratio is the single strongest predictor
    (OR 5.46), confirming an informative-missingness signal.}
    \label{table:coefs}
\end{table}

The P/F ratio measurement indicator alone has an odds ratio of \textbf{5.46}:
having this test ordered multiplies the odds of subsequent onset by more than
fivefold, independent of the test result. This finding quantifies a
\emph{label-recognition ceiling} \cite{groenwold2020missingness}: the onset
label depends on clinician suspicion (cultures and antibiotics), and the act of
ordering labs reflects that same suspicion. This is a property of the data,
not a model deficiency, and it sets an upper bound on what any retrospective
model can achieve (C4).

Figure~\ref{fig:calibration_h6} shows the out-of-sample calibration curve for
the Analysis-1 nomogram, demonstrating that predicted risks closely match observed
onset rates.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{images/10_calibration_h6.png}
    \caption{Calibration curve (out-of-sample) for the Analysis-1 elastic-net
    nomogram at the hour-six landmark. Points near the diagonal indicate
    that predicted risks are usable as probabilities, not merely as a ranking.}
    \label{fig:calibration_h6}
\end{figure}

\section{Analysis 2: Dynamic Landmark Supermodel (C1)}
\label{sec:supermodel_results}
%==========================================================================

The Analysis-2 supermodel is the central deliverable: a single elastic-net
logistic model fitted on 207{,}010 stacked landmark rows across 48{,}829
stays, with 56 features including landmark time $s$, $s^2$, and 27
feature-by-$s$ interactions. Patient-grouped five-fold cross-validation
produces out-of-sample predictions at every landmark.

\subsection{Overall Discrimination}
The pooled out-of-sample AUROC across all landmarks is
\textbf{0.797}. Table~\ref{table:auc_by_landmark} shows the discrimination
at each prediction hour, alongside a separate per-landmark model (no $s$

\subsection{Interpretation of the Dynamic Discrimination Pattern}

The AUC pattern across landmarks is clinically informative. Discrimination is
\textbf{highest early} (AUROC 0.77 at hour 6), when the pool of at-risk stays
is largest and the onset signal is strongest. It \textbf{dips in the mid-stay
window} (hours 18--24), where incident onset is rarest and hardest to predict
among the patients who have survived the initial hours without developing
sepsis. Discrimination then \textbf{recovers at later landmarks} (hours 36--48),
because the patients still at risk at these hours constitute a selected
subpopulation in which the remaining onset events carry a clearer signal.

This pattern is consistent with the epidemiology of incident sepsis: the
highest-risk patients develop onset early, leaving a mid-stay population with
a lower base rate and weaker signal, followed by a late-stay population where
prolonged ICU exposure concentrates risk factors.

\subsection{Time-Dependent AUC Across Horizons}

Figure~\ref{fig:timedep_auc} shows the time-dependent AUC for predicting
onset at increasing horizons from the hour-six landmark, using the
cross-validated multivariable risk score. Discrimination holds above 0.70
across all clinically relevant horizons, confirming that the model provides
useful risk stratification not only at the 12-hour window but also at longer
prediction horizons.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/09_timedep_auc.png}
    \caption{Time-dependent AUC for predicting sepsis onset over increasing
    horizons from the hour-six landmark. Discrimination remains above 0.70

\end{figure}

\section{Analysis 3: Within-Patient Correlation (GEE and Time-Varying Cox)}
%==========================================================================

The within-patient exchangeable correlation estimated by GEE is
\textbf{$\alpha = 0.676$}: hourly observations within a patient are strongly
dependent, and the effective information is far below the 76{,}538 row count.
Cluster-robust (sandwich) standard errors on the pooled logistic model inflate
by up to \textbf{4.5$\times$} compared to naive model-based standard errors
(Table~\ref{table:gee_se}). Without this correction, a pooled GLM would report
spuriously tight confidence intervals.

respiratory rate (HR 1.06) raise the hazard of onset, while MAP (HR 0.65)
lowers it. These are inferentially valid companions to the predictive
supermodel, not competitors to it.

\section{Analysis 4: Real-Time Evaluation (Utility and Alarm Burden)}
%==========================================================================

Discrimination alone is insufficient for a streaming alarm system. The
supermodel is evaluated on a held-out 6{,}000-stay set (trained on the
remainder, no leakage), scored at every hour from hour 6 to onset or
discharge.

\paragraph{PhysioNet utility.}
The PhysioNet/CinC 2019 normalised utility score, which rewards early
detection and penalises both late and false alarms, is maximised at
\textbf{0.289} (0 = never-alarm baseline, 1 = per-hour oracle) at a risk
threshold of 0.04. A positive score confirms that the model adds clinical
value over silence on this incident-only ICU task.

\paragraph{Alarm burden.}
Figure~\ref{fig:alarm_tradeoff} shows the sensitivity--alarm burden trade-off,
the clinically decisive picture for deployment.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/15_alarm_tradeoff.png}
    \caption{Alarm-burden trade-off for the real-time supermodel. High
    sensitivity ($\ge 90\%$) is achievable only at alarm rates (6.5 false
    alarms per patient-day) that would fatigue bedside staff. A practical
    operating point (51\% sensitivity, 0.7 false alarms per patient-day)
    balances detection against alarm fatigue.}
    \label{fig:alarm_tradeoff}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{table:alarm_ops} summarises three operating points that illustrate
the trade-off. Catching 90\% of septic patients in a timely window costs about

untenable alarm fatigue. A calmer operating point (51\% sensitivity) drops that
to 0.7 per patient-day, alarming only 30\% of non-septic patients. This is the
number a deployment decision actually turns on.

\section{Analysis 6: Group-Based Trajectory Modelling}
%==========================================================================

A latent-class growth model fitted to first-24h SOFA trajectories (2{,}438
stays with $\ge 12$ hourly observations) selects \textbf{three classes} by
BIC. Figure~\ref{fig:gbtm} shows the mean SOFA trajectory per class.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/18_gbtm_trajectories.png}
    \caption{GBTM: three latent SOFA-trajectory classes over the first 24 ICU
    hours. Class 1 (mildest) is flat near zero; Class 2 (intermediate) rises
    to SOFA $\sim$3; Class 3 (most severe) rises steeply to SOFA $\sim$7.
    The trajectory \emph{shape}, not just its endpoint, carries prognostic
    and predictive information.}
    \label{fig:gbtm}
\end{figure}

Class membership shows a strong, monotonic dose-response relationship with
both outcomes. For in-hospital mortality, the most-severe class (Class 3)
carries a hazard ratio of \textbf{5.019} ($p = 3.9 \times 10^{-8}$) relative to the
mildest class, adjusted for age (Table~\ref{table:gbtm_mortality}). For
incident Sepsis-3, the most-severe class carries an odds ratio of
\textbf{18.81} ($p = 1.5 \times 10^{-74}$), confirming that the SOFA trajectory
\emph{shape}, not just its terminal value, is informative of subsequent
onset (Table~\ref{table:gbtm_onset}).

\section{Analysis 7: Confounder-Adjusted Causal Analysis}
%==========================================================================

Every prior analysis reports an \emph{association} (a risk score, a hazard ratio).
Analysis~7 applies propensity-score matching (PSM), inverse-probability weighting
(IPW), and doubly robust augmented-IPW estimation to estimate the
confounder-adjusted effect of early antibiotic administration ($\le 3$ hours
of ICU admission) on in-hospital mortality among 24{,}203 Sepsis-3 patients
(48\% early, 18.7\% mortality). The adjustment set is selected by LASSO from
11 candidate confounders; all 11 survive penalisation.

Figure~\ref{fig:love_plot} shows the covariate balance before and after
propensity-score matching: every covariate's standardised mean difference
falls below the conventional 0.1 threshold after matching.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/19_analysis7_balance_love_plot.png}
    \caption{Love plot: covariate balance before (red) and after (teal)
    propensity-score matching. All standardised mean differences fall below
    0.1 after matching, confirming adequate balance.}
    \label{fig:love_plot}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{table:causal} summarises the effect estimates across all four
estimators.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{l|c|r|c}
        \hline
        \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Estimand} & \textbf{Odds ratio} & \textbf{95\% CI} \\
        \hline\hline

Agreement across four estimators built on different assumptions provides the
strongest evidence this observational pipeline can offer for a real
association: early antibiotics are associated with lower mortality. However,
as an observational analysis, it cannot rule out unmeasured confounders.

calibration curves on eICU. The frozen model over-predicts risk approximately
threefold (calibration slope 0.54, reflecting the lower onset base rate in
the multi-centre population), but a single-parameter intercept-and-slope
recalibration corrects this, reducing the Brier score from 0.052 to 0.040.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The frozen-transport AUROC (0.686) sits close to
the eICU-native refit ceiling (0.711), so most of the drop from MIMIC's 0.775
reflects a harder, more heterogeneous multi-centre population rather than
model failure. The DeLong test between frozen and refit is significant
($p < 10^{-27}$), confirming a modest but real gap that could be closed by
local recalibration. This is the expected result for any single-centre model
transported to 200+ ICUs, and it is consistent with the validation degradation
reported by Wang \emph{et al.} \cite{wang2025srpm} across the broader
literature.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
%==========================================================================

\subsection{Central Finding}

The central empirical finding of this thesis is that the ceiling on
incident-onset prediction is set by the \emph{label's dependence on clinician
recognition} (when cultures and antibiotics are ordered) rather than by model
class. This is why the strongest predictors are missingness indicators and why
a purely statistical model is competitive with more complex approaches: no
neural network is required to approach a ceiling imposed by the labelling
process itself. This result aligns with, and sharpens, the
informative-missingness literature
\cite{groenwold2020missingness,singh2021missingness}.

\subsection{Strengths}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Interpretability.} Every coefficient is a readable log-odds
  ratio or hazard ratio. Clinicians can trace the model's reasoning, and
  regulatory frameworks (e.g., the EU AI Act) favour transparency.
  \item \textbf{Assumption testing.} The proportional-hazards assumption was
  tested and, because it failed, a time-varying reformulation was fitted in
  response. This workflow is absent from the reviewed MIMIC-IV literature.
  \item \textbf{External validation.} The frozen-transport paradigm (no
  refitting) is the most honest form of external validation and directly
  tests the model's deployability.
  \item \textbf{Clinical framing.} The alarm-burden analysis translates
  discrimination into the metric that deployment decisions actually depend
  on.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

Several threats remain and are documented rather than hidden:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The onset label depends on documentation timing (when cultures and
  antibiotics are ordered); ``onset'' partly reflects clinical recognition
  rather than biological transition.
  \item The assumed-zero SOFA baseline may slightly overcount organ dysfunction
  in patients with chronic organ impairment.
  \item qSOFA uses mean arterial pressure (MAP) as a proxy for systolic blood
  pressure, which is not the original qSOFA definition.
  \item The time-varying Cox and GBTM analyses use sampled subsets (2{,}254 and
  2{,}438 stays respectively) for computational tractability.
  \item The external validation on eICU-CRD requires an adaptation in the
  suspected-infection trigger (antibiotics alone, with culture refinement when
  available) due to eICU's limited microbiology capture ($\sim$1.5\% of stays).
\end{itemize}
