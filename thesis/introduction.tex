\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Context and Motivation}
Sepsis, defined by the Sepsis-3 consensus as life-threatening organ dysfunction
caused by a dysregulated host response to infection \cite{singer2016sepsis3},
remains a leading cause of mortality in the intensive care unit (ICU). Globally,
sepsis accounts for approximately 11 million deaths annually, representing
nearly 20\% of all deaths worldwide. Because each hour of delayed treatment
increases mortality by a measurable margin, predicting the \emph{onset} of
sepsis ahead of clinical recognition is of direct clinical value: an early
warning, even by a few hours, can accelerate the initiation of antibiotics,
fluid resuscitation, and source control.

Two modelling traditions address this prediction problem. The first, and by far
the more actively published, is machine learning: gradient-boosted trees,
recurrent neural networks, and transformer architectures dominate the recent
literature. The second, older and still foundational, is classical statistics:
Cox proportional-hazards regression, logistic regression, generalised estimating
equations, and trajectory modelling. Statistical models retain specific
advantages for real-time clinical use that are often undervalued in the current
literature:

\begin{itemize}
  \item They yield directly interpretable hazard ratios and odds ratios that
  clinicians can relate to their bedside experience.
  \item Their assumptions (proportional hazards, linearity in the log-odds,
  independence of observations) can be formally tested, and the consequences of
  violation can be quantified and addressed.
  \item They can be benchmarked transparently against the established rule-based
  scores that clinicians already use: the Systemic Inflammatory Response
  Syndrome (SIRS) criteria, the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment
  (qSOFA), and the full SOFA score.
  \item Their coefficients are closed-form and portable: a model fitted on one
  database can be transported to another by applying the same linear predictor,
  without retraining or access to the development data.
\end{itemize}

On the MIMIC-IV database \cite{johnson2023mimiciv}---a large, de-identified
critical-care database from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center
containing over 65{,}000 adult ICU stays---the statistical literature has
developed unevenly. As detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:review}, most studies
predict mortality \emph{after} sepsis is diagnosed rather than onset itself;
the proportional-hazards assumption is rarely tested before a Cox model is
fitted; and external validation is largely absent from the onset-prediction
literature specifically. The single statistical real-time onset benchmark,
TREWScore \cite{henry2015trewscore}, was fitted on the older MIMIC-II database
in 2015 and has not been revisited on MIMIC-IV with purely statistical,
interpretable tools.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:gap}
Three specific gaps motivate this work and are established in detail through
the systematic review of Chapter~\ref{ch:review}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{G1 (Onset versus prognosis).} MIMIC-IV statistical work
  overwhelmingly predicts mortality after diagnosis. The dominant pattern
  couples LASSO variable selection with a logistic nomogram
  \cite{yuan2023nomogram}, and the trajectory literature uses GBTM to link SOFA
  shapes to mortality \cite{yang2022gbtm}. Genuine pre-onset, real-time
  detection with purely statistical tools has not been revisited on MIMIC-IV
  since TREWScore's 2015 MIMIC-II study \cite{henry2015trewscore}.

  \item \textbf{G2 (Untested proportional hazards).} Sepsis hazards are
  frequently non-proportional \cite{kasal2004cox}, yet no reviewed MIMIC-IV Cox
  study reports testing the assumption, weakening confidence in the reported
  hazard ratios. The appropriate response to a failed proportional-hazards
  test---a time-varying reformulation---is absent from the MIMIC-IV onset
  literature.

  \item \textbf{G3 (Validation asymmetry).} External validation is common in the
  prognostic nomogram literature but essentially absent from onset-prediction
  and trajectory studies. Wang \emph{et al.} \cite{wang2025srpm} reviewed 91
  sepsis real-time prediction models and found that the median AUROC falls from
  0.886 internally to 0.783 externally, demonstrating that the
  internal-to-external gap, rather than raw internal discrimination, is the
  field's central weakness. This gap is precisely where onset-prediction models
  are most vulnerable, yet precisely where validation is most lacking.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Question and Objectives}
\label{sec:rq}
These gaps motivate one primary research question and three sub-questions.

\vspace{4pt}
\noindent\textbf{RQ.} \emph{Can a purely statistical (Cox proportional-hazards /
time-varying / landmark) model, using routinely available real-time vitals and
laboratory values in MIMIC-IV, predict impending Sepsis-3 onset ahead of
clinical recognition, with performance comparable to or exceeding the rule-based
scores SIRS, qSOFA, and SOFA?}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{SQ1.} Which routinely measured variables are the strongest
  statistically significant predictors of incident onset, and does the
  proportional-hazards assumption hold for the fitted Cox model?
  \item \textbf{SQ2.} Does modelling trajectories through a time-varying or
  dynamic landmark extension improve early detection over a static
  single-time-point model?
  \item \textbf{SQ3.} How does discriminative performance compare with SIRS,
  qSOFA, and SOFA on the correct incident-onset task, and does it survive the
  transition from internal (MIMIC-IV) to external (eICU-CRD) evaluation?
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contrib}
This thesis makes the following contributions, each mapped to the gap it
closes:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{C1: First interpretable statistical real-time Sepsis-3 onset
  model on MIMIC-IV (closes G1).} A dynamic landmark supermodel
  \cite{vanhouwelingen2007landmark,fries2023dynamiclm} is fitted across six
  landmark hours $\{6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48\}$ with feature-by-time interactions,
  yielding one interpretable model that emits continuously updated hourly risk.
  Incident sepsis is explicitly separated from prevalent sepsis using an
  hour-six landmark, and the model reaches an overall pooled out-of-sample
  AUROC of 0.797 across all landmarks.

  \item \textbf{C2: Explicit proportional-hazards testing with a triggered
  time-varying reformulation (closes G2).} The Schoenfeld residual test is
  applied to the onset Cox model; because the global test fails ($p < 10^{-16}$),
  a time-varying Cox model on a counting-process formulation is fitted in direct
  response, demonstrating a workflow that the MIMIC-IV literature has omitted.

  \item \textbf{C3: Reframing the benchmark.} On the correct incident-onset
  target, standard bedside scores are non-discriminative to inverse: SOFA at
  hour 6 achieves an AUROC of only 0.36, qSOFA 0.49, and SIRS 0.59. A
  multivariable landmark nomogram attains 0.775, demonstrating that severity
  scores detect \emph{established} organ failure, not \emph{impending} onset.

  \item \textbf{C4: Informative-missingness characterisation.} Binary ``was
  this lab ordered'' indicators are the strongest predictors in the elastic-net
  nomogram \cite{groenwold2020missingness,singh2021missingness}. The P/F ratio
  measurement indicator alone carries an odds ratio of 5.46, quantifying a
  label-recognition ceiling intrinsic to retrospectively labelled ICU data.

  \item \textbf{C5: A reproducible, transportable pipeline that is externally
  validated on eICU-CRD (closes G3).} The frozen MIMIC model is applied
  without refitting to the eICU Collaborative Research Database
  \cite{pollard2018eicu} (113{,}597 at-risk stays, 200+ US ICUs).
  Discrimination transports at AUROC 0.686 against an internal eICU ceiling of
  0.712, and a single-parameter recalibration corrects the expected
  miscalibration.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Structure}
The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} sets out the research methodology, data sources,
cohort construction, and evaluation framework.
Chapter~\ref{ch:review} reviews the related statistical and machine-learning
literature and formalises the three gaps above.
Chapter~\ref{ch:design} describes the system architecture and the design of
each pipeline stage, from data extraction through the seven statistical tiers.
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} presents and critically discusses the internal and
external results, with particular emphasis on the dynamic landmark supermodel
(Tier~2), which is the central methodological contribution of this work.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes with a summary of findings and avenues
for further research.
