\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter presents the results in seven sections: cohort description, model
performance and calibration, clinical utility (net benefit, operating points
and event-level detection), variance decomposition, treatment-anchored
evaluation, external validation, and equity analysis. All results are from
pre-registered analyses unless marked as exploratory.

\subsection{Key Finding: Every Model Fails Clinically}

Every model under every label variant achieves a \textbf{negative} Utility
Score. This is the primary empirical finding. It means that every system
tested (GBT, the primary model, NEWS2, qSOFA, SIRS, and the static Cox
model) performs worse than giving no alerts at all.

Alert burdens range from 91 false alerts per true alert (GBT, Variant C) to
over 340 (Cox and qSOFA, Variants A/B), compared with the published benchmark
of 1.4 \cite{moor2023review}. The primary model issues no true alerts
at all ($\infty$ alert burden) under the default threshold.

This is consistent with Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2025srpm}: the
internal-to-external utility collapse they found across ninety-one models
already appears \textit{internally} on MIMIC-IV under an honest time-based
split. The failure is only visible because the Utility Score is reported;
AUROC alone would hide it.

Two qualifications apply, both developed in
Section~\ref{sec:clinical_utility}. First, these figures are computed at a
default 0.5 cut-off, which is not an operating point any deployment would
choose; at a threshold matched to the event prevalence the primary model does
achieve positive net benefit over both default strategies
(Table~\ref{tab:net_benefit}). Second, the Utility Score and net benefit
disagree here because they price false alarms differently, and that
disagreement is itself evidence for H5 rather than a reason to prefer one
number. The conclusion that survives both metrics is narrower than ``every
model fails'': no configuration tested reaches an alert burden a ward could
absorb, and only one of the six models can be tuned by threshold at all.

\subsection{Calibration}

Calibration intercept and slope are two summary numbers describing a whole
curve, so both the curve itself and a scalar summary of its distance from the
diagonal are reported. Following Van Calster et al.\
\cite{vancalster2019calibration}, the flexible calibration curve is a
restricted cubic spline of the outcome on the logit of predicted risk, and it
is summarised by the Integrated Calibration Index (ICI, the mean absolute
distance between predicted risk and that curve) and the scaled Brier score,
$1 - \text{Brier}/\text{Brier}_{\text{null}}$, where the null model predicts
the prevalence for every person-hour.

Two results stand out. First, only the primary model is calibrated at all.
Under Variants A and C its intercept is within 0.09 of zero, its slope within
0.10 of one, and its ICI is below 0.0005 --- roughly a quarter of the 0.2 per
cent event rate. Its scaled Brier score is positive, though barely
($+0.004$ and $+0.008$), meaning it improves on the null model by less than
one per cent.

Second, GBT and Cox are not merely imperfectly calibrated; they are
miscalibrated by roughly three orders of magnitude. Both carry intercepts near
$-6$, and their scaled Brier scores range from $-47$ to $-143$. A scaled Brier
of $-91.6$ means the GBT model's squared error is 92.6 times worse than simply
predicting the prevalence for every person-hour. Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}
shows why: GBT emits predicted risks between 0.2 and 0.9 for person-hours
whose observed event rate never exceeds 0.04. This matters directly for
Section~\ref{sec:clinical_utility}: a model whose probabilities are inflated
by two orders of magnitude cannot be operated at any threshold a clinician
would choose.

Variant B is the exception among the primary models, with a slope of 0.209 ---
the model over-spreads its predictions by roughly a factor of five. Its ICI
(0.0027) exceeds its own E90 (0.0016), which happens only when a small number
of extreme predictions dominate the mean; the Variant B model does in fact
emit predicted probabilities of exactly 1.0 for a handful of person-hours.

\subsubsection{A Correction to the Clamped Estimates}

The calibration intercepts and slopes above supersede those produced by the
metric-suite notebook, which computed them after clamping predicted
probabilities to $[0.001, 0.999]$. That guard is standard for models whose
predictions approach 0 or 1, but at a 0.2 per cent hourly event rate it is
actively harmful: 45\% (Variant A), 72\% (Variant B) and 7.5\% (Variant C) of
the primary model's predicted probabilities fall \emph{below} the clamp floor,
so the clamp was overwriting the majority of the quantity being measured. The
values in Table~\ref{tab:calibration} use a floor of $10^{-6}$, below the
smallest non-zero prediction in the test set. The correction moves the
Variant A slope from 1.169 to 1.029 and the Variant B slope from 0.318 to
0.209; the qualitative conclusions are unchanged, but the Variant A model is
better calibrated, and the Variant B model worse, than previously reported.
This is recorded here rather than silently patched, because it is exactly the
kind of scale-dependent analyst decision this study set out to measure.

\section{Clinical Utility: Net Benefit, Operating Points, and Event-Level
    Detection}
\label{sec:clinical_utility}
%===========================================================================

AUROC, AUPRC and the Utility Score describe a model; they do not describe what
a ward would experience if the model were switched on. This section reports
the four quantities that do: net benefit across the range of thresholds a
clinician might plausibly accept, the positive predictive value and false-alarm
rate at a fixed sensitivity, detection expressed per patient rather than per
person-hour, and the lead time gained when detection occurs.

One framing point governs everything below. Under the discrete-time
competing-risks specification, $\text{event}=1$ marks the sepsis onset hour
itself, so each septic stay contributes exactly one event hour out of a mean
of 45. Per-hour sensitivity therefore demands that the alert fire \emph{in the
onset hour}; per-patient sensitivity asks only whether the patient was alerted
at all before onset. The two are different questions, and reporting only the
first, as the metric suite in Section~\ref{sec:internal_performance} does,
understates detection while hiding the alarm volume that buys it.

\subsection{Decision-Curve Analysis}

Decision-curve analysis, pre-specified in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, requires a choice of
threshold grid, and at this prevalence that choice determines the entire
result. A threshold probability of $t$ encodes a willingness to accept
$(1-t)/t$ false alarms per true detection. The hourly event rate here is
0.19--0.42 per cent, so the conventional grid of 0.01--0.20 sits between five
and one hundred times the prevalence: at $t=0.01$ the analyst is asserting a
tolerance of only 99 false alarms per detection, far below what an hourly
sepsis alert must absorb, and every curve falls trivially below ``treat
none''. Net benefit is therefore evaluated on a grid running from one tenth to
ten times the prevalence, following Vickers and Elkin \cite{vickers2006dca}.
The first run of the metric suite used the conventional grid and produced no
usable curve; that null result is a property of the grid, not of the models.

Read on the correct threshold scale, decision-curve analysis gives the most
favourable result in this thesis --- and it is still a weak one. At a
threshold equal to the prevalence, the primary model captures 38.0\% (Variant
A) and 39.9\% (Variant C) of the attainable net benefit, and 15.0\% under
Variant B. This is genuine, if modest, value over both defaults, and it sits
awkwardly beside the uniformly negative Utility Scores in
Table~\ref{tab:internal_results}. The two metrics disagree because they price
errors differently: net benefit weights a false alarm by $t/(1-t)$, which at
$t=0.002$ is almost nothing, whereas the Utility Score imposes a fixed
$-0.05$ penalty per false-alert hour regardless of threshold. Which is right
is a clinical judgement, not a statistical one, and the divergence is itself
an instance of H5.

The alert-rate span column carries the more damaging finding. For every model
except the primary one it is exactly zero: across the entire plausible
threshold range, the alert rate does not move. GBT and Cox alert on
\emph{100\% of person-hours} at every threshold in this range, so their
decision curves are
not merely poor but identical to treating everyone --- the green GBT line in
Figure~\ref{fig:decision_curves} sits exactly on the treat-all reference.
This is the direct consequence of the calibration failure in
Table~\ref{tab:calibration}: with predicted probabilities inflated to the
0.2--0.9 range, no threshold below 4 per cent excludes anything. NEWS2, qSOFA
and SIRS are non-tunable for the opposite reason --- their integer scores map
to a handful of fixed probabilities, all above the grid --- and alert on a
constant 58--86 per cent of person-hours. The primary model is the only one of
the six that can be operated as a threshold-based alert at all.

At 80 per cent sensitivity, PPV ranges from 0.0021 to 0.0074. The best case,
the primary model under the Liberal label, requires 135 alerted person-hours
per true event hour; the worst, the same model under the Seymour-Standard
label, requires 466. Every configuration generates between 10.9 and 17.0
false-alert hours per patient-day. A twelve-bed unit at the best operating
point would field roughly 130 false alert-hours per day.

Setting the threshold this way also exposes a limitation of the coarse
rule-based scores that the ratio-based alert burden concealed. SIRS cannot be
tuned to 80 per cent sensitivity under any variant, and neither can qSOFA
under the Liberal label: with only four distinct score values, the lowest
cut-off reaching the target is the one that alerts on every person-hour
(sensitivity 1.000, specificity 0.000, 23.9 false-alert hours per patient-day
--- an alert on every hour of every stay). qSOFA does reach the target under
Variants A and B, but only at specificity 0.344 and 15.7 false-alert hours per
patient-day. Their apparent competitiveness on AUROC in
Table~\ref{tab:internal_results} does not survive being asked to hit a
sensitivity target.

overlap the window from six hours before to three hours after onset, so a
five-hour alert run counts once rather than five times.

Read alone, the first two columns look like a success: 85--95 per cent of
septic patients are alerted inside the actionable six-hour window, and
88--100 per cent are alerted at some point before onset. This is the number a
paper reporting only per-patient detection would headline.

The fifth column shows what it costs. To achieve it, the models alert on
82.9--100 per cent of \emph{all} stays. Patient-level PPV is consequently

whether a patient will become septic, because essentially every patient is
alerted. The primary model under Variants A and C alerts on 100.0 per cent of
stays: not one of the 12{,}132 test patients would go through the ICU without
triggering it.

by a factor of twenty-six, which is larger than the spread this thesis
attributes to label definition (H1) or model class (H6). Denominator choice
belongs on the list of analyst decisions that require pre-specification.

\subsection{H1: Label Dominance (Confirmed)}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUSand!30, linecolor=ATUGreen]
    \textbf{H1:} Spread in AUROC across label variants $\geq$ spread across model
    classes.

    \textbf{Result:} Label spread = 0.153; model spread = 0.140.
    \textbf{H1 CONFIRMED.}
\end{mdframed}

Changing the sepsis definition while keeping the model fixed produces slightly
more AUROC variation (0.153) than changing the model while keeping the label
fixed (0.140). This confirms Cohen et al.'s \cite{cohen2024subtle} finding
on MIMIC-IV: the label definition contributes at least as much to reported
performance as the model choice. The margin is narrow (0.013), meaning both
sources are comparable, not that one clearly dominates.

The sign instability of MAP, temperature, and vasopressor indicators is

\subsection{H3: Treatment Leakage, Label Constitutionality (Confirmed)}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUPurple!10, linecolor=ATUPurple]
    \textbf{H3:} Restricting to pre-treatment hours reduces AUROC by $\geq 0.03$.

    \textbf{Result:} \textbf{0 sepsis onset events} in 179,003 pre-treatment
    person-hours. The prediction target is entirely absent.
    \textbf{FINDING SUPERSEDES HYPOTHESIS.}
\end{mdframed}

When only hours before the first antibiotic or culture are considered, there
are zero sepsis onset events across 179,003 person-hours, for every variant
and every model. This is not a small drop in performance. The prediction target
simply does not exist in the clinically honest time window.

No patient develops sepsis before doctors begin the clinical actions (ordering
antibiotics and cultures) that define sepsis. This confirms Kamran et al.'s
\cite{kamran2024evaluation} argument at the structural level: the Sepsis-3
label on MIMIC-IV is \textbf{entirely constituted by clinical action}. Every
published MIMIC-IV sepsis prediction result that does not restrict to the
pre-treatment window is evaluating whether the model can predict when a
clinician will act, not when a patient will deteriorate.

\subsection{H4: Split Optimism (Confirmed)}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUSand!30, linecolor=ATUGreen]
    \textbf{H4:} Temporal-split AUROC is at least 0.02 lower than random-split
    AUROC.

    \textbf{Result:} Primary model: random AUROC = 0.730, temporal AUROC = 0.607.
    Difference: 0.123. \textbf{H4 CONFIRMED.}
\end{mdframed}

For the primary model, the random split inflates AUROC by \textbf{0.123}

appear much better than it would perform in practice. This confirms Guo et

The GBT model shows a smaller, reversed pattern (temporal 0.746 vs.\ random

the linear model. However, both models have negative Utility Scores regardless
of split method.

\subsection{H5: Metric Divergence (Confirmed)}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUSand!30, linecolor=ATUGreen]
    \textbf{H5:} Utility Score declines proportionally more than AUROC from
    internal to external validation.

    \textbf{Result:} The Utility Score is already negative internally.
    \textbf{H5 CONFIRMED (qualitatively stronger).}
\end{mdframed}

Wang et al.'s \cite{wang2025srpm} finding, that AUROC understates
how badly models fail externally, is confirmed in a stronger form: the
Utility Score is already negative \textit{internally}. The ``crossing zero''
event that Wang et al.\ documented at the external stage has already occurred
before the model leaves MIMIC-IV.

\subsection{H6: Model-Class Null (Rejected)}

\begin{mdframed}[backgroundcolor=ATUOrange!20, linecolor=ATUOrange]
    \textbf{H6:} $|\Delta_\text{AUROC}(\text{primary} - \text{GBT})| \leq 0.02$.

    \textbf{Result:} $\Delta = 0.133$ (GBT: 0.740, primary: 0.607).
    \textbf{H6 REJECTED.}
\end{mdframed}

GBT outperforms the primary model by 0.133 AUROC under the Seymour-Standard
label, far exceeding the 0.02 non-inferiority threshold. The interpretable
model carries a substantial AUROC cost.

But the ranking reverses on clinical utility: GBT has Utility Score $-5.112$
while the primary model has $-1.004$. The model with higher AUROC produces
more harmful false alerts. Which model is ``better'' depends entirely on
which metric is used.

\section{Proportional-Hazards Testing}
\label{sec:schoenfeld}
%===========================================================================

The Schoenfeld residual test on the static Cox model (Variant B) decisively
rejects proportional hazards ($\chi^2 = 171.8$, df = 11,
$p = 5.0 \times 10^{-31}$). Five covariates violate the assumption
individually: temperature ($p = 2.4 \times 10^{-12}$), GCS
($p = 5.2 \times 10^{-19}$), creatinine ($p = 8.9 \times 10^{-5}$),
bilirubin ($p = 7.6 \times 10^{-6}$), and platelets ($p = 0.002$). This
confirms that the static Cox model is fundamentally wrong for this data and
justifies the discrete-time formulation as the primary model.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%===========================================================================

\subsection{The Core Finding}

The most important finding is structural, not numerical: in the pre-treatment
window, the Sepsis-3 label on MIMIC-IV contains zero events before clinical
action begins. This means every published MIMIC-IV sepsis prediction result
evaluates the model's ability to predict clinician behaviour, not physiological
deterioration. The Utility Score is negative for all models because the
temporal split exposes the model to a test population where the same
treatment-related signals do not rescue performance as consistently as in the
training years. AUROC survives because the ordinal ranking of patients is
preserved even as absolute calibration breaks.

\subsection{H1 and H6 Together}

H1 (label dominance) and H6 (model-class null) are in productive tension, as
designed. H1 is narrowly confirmed (label spread 0.153, model spread 0.140);
H6 is decisively rejected (GBT gap 0.133). Both the label and the model
contribute large, comparable variance. The conventional literature, which
typically fixes the label and reports model comparisons, is measuring one
source of variance while ignoring another of similar size.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-centre development.} All models were developed on
    MIMIC-IV, which originates from a single academic medical centre (Beth
    Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston). Clinical practices, patient
    demographics, and EHR documentation patterns at BIDMC may not represent
    those at other institutions. The equity and label-sensitivity findings in
    particular should not be assumed to transfer to hospitals with different
    patient populations or antibiotic prescribing cultures. The eICU-CRD
    external validation provides some evidence of generalisability, but the
    small event count (63 sepsis onsets) limits the strength of that evidence.

    \item \textbf{Small external event count.} The 63 labelled sepsis events in
    eICU-CRD are too few for reliable Utility Score or AUPRC evaluation. The
    external results primarily confirm the label-dependent generalisation
    pattern (Variant B collapses; A and C hold).

    \item \textbf{Deep survival model not implemented.} A deep survival
    comparator was planned but not built. This does not affect the structural
    findings about the label.

    \item \textbf{Limited feature set.} Clinical notes, procedure codes, and
    ventilator settings were not included. Richer features might improve
    discrimination but would not change the treatment-constitutionality finding.

    \item \textbf{Specific to Sepsis-3.} These findings apply to the Sepsis-3
    definition on MIMIC-IV. Other sepsis definitions or databases may behave
    differently.

    \item \textbf{Operating points are not externally recalibrated.} The
    thresholds in Section~\ref{sec:clinical_utility} are derived on the test
    set at which they are evaluated, so the reported PPV and false-alarm rates
    are optimistic: a prospectively fixed threshold would perform no better.
    The external operating points in Table~\ref{tab:external_utility} are
    likewise re-derived on eICU rather than transferred from MIMIC-IV, which
    isolates the discrimination loss but hides the additional loss a
    transferred threshold would incur.

    \item \textbf{Alarm episodes are defined by contiguity, not by
    acknowledgement.} False-alarm episodes are counted as contiguous runs of
    alert hours. A real system would apply suppression, snoozing and
    escalation logic, all of which would reduce the episode counts in
    Table~\ref{tab:patient_level}. The alert-hour rates are the conservative
    bound and the episode rates the optimistic one; the true burden lies
    between them.
\end{enumerate}
