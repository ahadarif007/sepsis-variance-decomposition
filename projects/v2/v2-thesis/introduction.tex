\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Context and Motivation}

Sepsis --- defined by the Sepsis-3 consensus as life-threatening organ dysfunction
caused by a dysregulated host response to infection \parencite{singer2016sepsis3}
--- kills an estimated eleven million people annually and accounts for nearly
20\% of all deaths worldwide \parencite{rudd2020global}. In the intensive care
unit (ICU), each additional hour before effective treatment is associated with
measurably higher mortality \parencite{seymour2017time}. An early warning system
that identifies impending sepsis onset ahead of clinical recognition could
therefore accelerate initiation of antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and source
control at the hour when intervention is most impactful.

The incentive has attracted substantial machine-learning investment. Wang et al.\
\parencite{wang2025srpm} catalogued ninety-one published real-time sepsis
prediction models and found a median internal AUROC of 0.811. Yet the same
review revealed that the median clinical Utility Score --- a metric that rewards
early true alerts and penalises late, missed, and false alerts --- collapsed from
$+0.381$ internally to $-0.164$ externally: from useful to categorically worse
than issuing no alert at all. AUROC failed to detect this collapse; in most
studies it barely moved. This pattern has since been confirmed in deployment:
Wong et al.\ \parencite{wong2026epic} evaluated the Epic Sepsis Model v2 across
227{,}091 encounters and reported AUROC between 0.82 and 0.92 alongside an alert
burden that the clinical staff described as unmanageable.

The question this collapse raises is not ``which model should we prefer?'' but
something more fundamental: \textit{how much of the apparent predictive performance
of a real-time sepsis model is attributable to the model itself, and how much to
the analyst's discretionary choices about how to define the outcome, how to split
the data, and which metric to report?} Wang et al.\ \parencite{wang2025srpm}
and Cohen et al.\ \parencite{cohen2024subtle} together suggest that the answer
is unsettling: most of the variance lives in the choices, not in the model.
Cohen et al.\ showed on MIMIC-III that the identified sepsis cohort ranged from
867 to 2{,}178 admissions depending solely on how Sepsis-3 was operationalised,
and that label sensitivity swung predictive performance at least as much as the
choice of model. Kamran et al.\ \parencite{kamran2024evaluation} showed that a
substantial portion of the apparent performance of published models reflects
the model learning to predict when a clinician will begin treatment ---
not independent physiological deterioration.

This thesis inverts the conventional design. Rather than holding the label,
the split, and the metric fixed and asking which model wins, it promotes all
three to \textbf{objects of study} and delivers a variance decomposition: a
quantitative answer to the question of how much each analyst choice contributes
to the spread in apparent performance. The contribution does not depend on any
model achieving a performance target; it is informative whichever way the
hypotheses resolve.

\section{Limitations of the Conventional Approach}

The conventional approach to real-time sepsis prediction --- as exemplified by the
majority of the ninety-one models catalogued by Wang et al.\
\parencite{wang2025srpm} --- treats the Sepsis-3 label as fixed, uses a single
operationalisation of the onset definition, employs a single train/test split
strategy (typically random), and reports AUROC as the primary metric. A dynamic
landmark supermodel fitted under these standard assumptions on MIMIC-IV can
achieve a pooled out-of-sample AUROC above 0.75 with eICU-CRD external validation
near 0.69. The proportional-hazards assumption is typically rejected (global
Schoenfeld $p < 10^{-16}$), and informative-missingness indicators frequently
outweigh the test values themselves.

These findings are sound as far as they go, but the extended literature review
(Chapter~\ref{ch:review}) reveals that each of those fixed choices --- label
operationalisation, split design, metric selection --- is a source of variance
large enough to change the substantive conclusion. A study that reports a single
label and a single metric is not a variance decomposition; it is a data point in
one cell of the decomposition table.

This thesis fills the remaining cells.

\section{Research Question and Pre-registered Hypotheses}
\label{sec:rq}

\noindent\textbf{Primary research question (RQ).} \textit{In real-time sepsis
onset prediction on MIMIC-IV, how is predictive performance and inferential
validity partitioned between (a) the choice of model class and (b) the analyst's
discretionary methodological choices: label operationalisation, temporal versus
random validation split, prediction-time anchoring relative to clinical action,
and choice of evaluation metric?}

The study is structured around six pre-registered hypotheses:

\begin{description}
  \item[H1 (Label dominance)] The spread in AUROC across the three label variants,
  holding the model and split fixed, is at least as large as the spread across
  model classes, holding the label and split fixed.
  \item[H2 (Coefficient instability)] At least one covariate changes sign or
  changes magnitude by a factor $\geq 2\times$ across label variants under the
  primary model.
  \item[H3 (Treatment leakage)] Restricting evaluation to the pre-treatment
  window --- hours before the first culture order or antibiotic --- reduces AUROC
  by at least 0.03, exposing the degree to which the label is constituted by
  clinical action.
  \item[H4 (Split optimism)] Temporal-split AUROC is at least 0.02 lower than
  random-split AUROC under the same model and label.
  \item[H5 (Metric divergence)] The proportional decline in performance from
  internal to external validation is substantially larger for the Utility Score
  than for AUROC; the Utility Score may cross zero.
  \item[H6 (Model-class null)] The primary discrete-time model is not inferior
  to gradient-boosted trees by more than 0.02 AUROC under a fixed label and
  temporal split.
\end{description}

H1 and H6 are in productive tension: if H1 is confirmed, the label is the
dominant source of variance; if H6 is rejected, the model class also matters.
Both outcomes are informative.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contrib}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{C1 (Label variance quantification).} The first pre-specified
  three-variant label sensitivity analysis for a dynamic discrete-time
  competing-risks model on MIMIC-IV, following Cohen et al.'s \parencite{cohen2024subtle}
  MIMIC-III protocol and Dutta et al.'s \parencite{dutta2026performance}
  prospective extension.
  \item \textbf{C2 (Treatment-constitutionality of the label).} A treatment-anchored
  evaluation that reveals the Sepsis-3 label contains zero predictable events in
  the pre-treatment window, confirming Kamran et al.'s \parencite{kamran2024evaluation}
  constitutionality finding on MIMIC-IV.
  \item \textbf{C3 (Utility collapse documented under the survival framework).}
  The PhysioNet Utility Score is negative for every model and every label
  variant, extending Wang et al.'s \parencite{wang2025srpm} cross-study finding
  to a controlled within-study comparison.
  \item \textbf{C4 (Coefficient instability across labels).} Formal documentation
  that MAP, temperature, bilirubin, platelet count, and vasopressor indicators
  change sign or magnitude across label variants, validating Lauritsen et al.'s
  \parencite{lauritsen2021framing} framing-instability claim for the MIMIC-IV
  hourly regime.
  \item \textbf{C5 (Equity-by-label analysis).} The conjunction of subgroup
  performance disparities and subgroup label-sensitivity variation, which has
  not been reported for any sepsis model, following Wang, Li, Naidech and Luo
  \parencite{wang2022equity}.
  \item \textbf{C6 (Transparent, open pipeline).} A fully reproducible,
  pre-registered R/Python pipeline released under a permissive licence, following
  TRIPOD+AI \parencite{collins2024tripodai} and PROBAST+AI
  \parencite{moons2025probastai} reporting standards.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Structure}

Chapter~\ref{ch:review} reviews the sources that motivated the design inversion
and maps each evidence strand to the design decision it constrains.
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} specifies the data sources, cohort criteria, label
variants, model classes, evaluation framework, and the pre-registration protocol.
Chapter~\ref{ch:design} describes the pipeline architecture --- thirteen numbered
scripts and the data artefacts they produce.
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} presents all empirical results, organised around the
six pre-registered hypotheses.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} draws the substantive conclusion and maps avenues for
further research.
